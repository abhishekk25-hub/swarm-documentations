Ely, and Eric White Abstract Research and testing within the Air Force Research Laboratory at Edwards Air Force Base in the southwestern Mojave Desert has been conducted since the 1950s. This testing requires large quantities of groundwater for cooling and operations involving hazardous constituents that have entered the groundwater. To better understand the potential movement of contaminants through the groundwater system, the U.S. Geological Survey entered into a cooperative agreement with the U.S. Air Force Civil Engineer Center with the purpose of (1) gaining a more detailed understanding of the hydraulic connection between the unconsolidated basin- fill sediment and the underlying weathered and crystalline bedrock and (2) investigating faults and their potential effect on groundwater flow. This study was specific to the Arroyos subarea and Northeast Air Force Research Laboratory subarea of the Air Force Research Laboratory. The refined hydrogeologic characterization was derived by evaluating surface geologic maps, a regional gravity model, new subsurface geophysical surveys, lithology data from boreholes, and groundwater- level data. Refinement of the hydrogeologic characterization of the study area involved developing a map of basin- fill sediment thickness, estimating the geometry and depth to bedrock, determining the presence of weathered bedrock material, and identifying the location of faults and subsurface structures. The thickness of basin- fill sediment was derived from a regional gravity model. Basin- fill sediment ranges from less than 20 feet (ft) thick to about 150 ft thick and increases substantially toward a deep structural basin in the northwestern part of the study area. The top of bedrock estimated from the new surface geophysical surveys is generally deeper than the top of bedrock from the regional gravity model. These differences in interpreted bedrock tops indicate that there may be a zone of weathered bedrock in the subsurface, overlying crystalline bedrock. Additional subsurface data evaluating the geometry and thickness of weathered bedrock are necessary for a comprehensive evaluation of weathered bedrock in the study area. Previously unmapped extensions of the Leuhman Fault, Spring Fault Zone, and Boron Gate Fault were identified in the new surface geophysical surveys. Evaluation of groundwater flow between bedrock and basin- fill sediment, and the effects of faults on groundwater flow, was done using groundwater- level data from 2020. The groundwater table was in crystalline bedrock throughout most of the Arroyos and Northeast Air Force Research Laboratory subareas, with a few exceptions where it was in basin- fill sediment and weathered bedrock. Basin- fill sediment, and likely weathered bedrock, was partially saturated in parts of the study area where basin- fill sediment was as thick as 150 ft. Weathered bedrock material may be present near the Spring Fault Zone, and if so, was likely saturated in 2020. The groundwater- level data near and within Spring Fault Zone indicate that the Spring Fault Zone acts as a vertical- along- strike conduit for, and as an across- strike impediment to, groundwater flow. In addition, steep groundwater- level gradients in the southeastern part of the study area indicate that a subsurface structure affecting groundwater flow could be related to the Leuhman Fault. Sufficient groundwater- level data were not available to conclusively determine if the Arroyos, Rich, and Boron Gate Faults, and their inferred extensions from the geophysical surveys, affect the movement or direction of groundwater flow. 2 Hydrogeologic Conceptualization of the Arroyos and NEAFRL Subareas, Edwards AFB, Antelope Valley, 2017–21 Introduction The Air Force Research Laboratory (AFRL) at Edwards Air Force Base (Edwards AFB; fig. 1) has been used as a rocket- research and testing facility since the 1950s. Research at the AFRL leads to the discovery, development, and integration of affordable warfighting technologies for the air, space, and cyberspace forces of the United States (U.S. Air Force, 2022). These research activities require the use of large quantities of groundwater for cooling and operations, as well as some hazardous constituents, including tetrachloroethylene, trichloroethylene, dichloroethylene, perchlorate, and N-nitrosodimethylamine (AECOM, 2013a). As of the time of publication, the use and disposal of these substances are regulated. However, in the past, these materials were spilled or released on the ground surface and infiltrated the subsurface, thus contaminating the local groundwater (AECOM, 2013a). A preliminary assessment of known and suspected hazardous substance sites was initiated at Edwards AFB in 1980 as part of the Air Force Installation Restoration Program (Tetra Tech, 2014). In 1989, the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency listed Edwards AFB on the National Priorities List under the Comprehensive Environmental Response, Compensation, and Liability Act (U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, 2021), thereby identifying the base as a site for long- term cleanup with oversight of the restoration activities provided by the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, the California Department of Toxic Substances Control, and the California Lahontan Regional Water Quality Control Board. Most water for Edwards AFB is supplied by the Antelope Valley- East Kern Water Agency, but some water for Edwards AFB is supplied by wells in the southern and western parts of the base (Edwards Air Force Base, 2021). All wells that extract groundwater for the base are in the Antelope Valley groundwater basin (basin number 6- 044; fig. 1; California Department of Water Resources, 2021). The regional direction of groundwater flow in the Antelope Valley groundwater basin is generally from the southwest to the northeast (Siade and others, 2014); however, groundwater- flow directions vary locally based on geologic structure and topography. Depth to groundwater at the north end of Rogers Dry Lake, which is the topographically lowest part of the lakebed, was less than 25 feet (ft) below land surface (bls) in 1957 (Durbin, 1978). In 2022, the depth to groundwater had increased to almost 110 ft bls (Dick and Teague, 2022). Understanding groundwater flow in the AFRL is essential to understanding the potential movement of contaminants through the groundwater system and the potential for future remediation activities, particularly in the two northern subareas of the AFRL, the Arroyos and Northeast AFRL (NEAFRL) subareas (fig. 1). Groundwater in the AFRL recharges along Leuhman Ridge and flows to the northwest and northeast off the ridge. In 2022, the depth to groundwater in the AFRL ranged from about 24 to 250 ft bls (Tetra Tech, 2022; Sustainment and Restoration Services, 2023). The AFRL’s groundwater system is composed of crystalline bedrock that is hydraulically connected with the overlying unconsolidated basin- fill sediment (AECOM, 2013a). The geologic structure is complex, with numerous unmapped or poorly documented faults that have the potential to affect groundwater flow. Understanding the geometry of the bedrock and how groundwater flows within and between the basin- fill sediment and bedrock, especially the transition zone interface between the two units, is important for long- term monitoring and management of groundwater in the AFRL (AECOM, 2013a). The U.S. Geological Survey (USGS) entered into a cooperative agreement with the U.S. Air Force Civil Engineer Center with the purpose of (1) gaining a more detailed understanding of the hydraulic connection between the unconsolidated basin- fill sediment and the underlying weathered and crystalline bedrock and (2) investigating faults and their potential effect on groundwater flow. This study began in 2017 with the collection of regional relative- gravity data (Langenheim and others, 2019) and borehole geophysical data (U.S. Geological Survey, 2023). Surface geophysical surveys using electrical resistivity tomography (ERT), time- domain electromagnetic (TEM), and horizontal- vertical spectral ratio (HVSR) passive seismic surveys were collected in 2018 (Groover and others, 2020). In 2020, a seismic reflection imaging survey and additional ERT data were collected near the Spring Fault Zone and Leuhman Fault to identify more precisely their surface and subsurface locations (Ely and Fenton, 2022; Catchings and others, 2023). A surface geological mapping study was also done to map the local geology and to investigate the locations of the Spring Fault Zone, Leuhman Fault, and surface lineaments identified from previously collected light detection and ranging (lidar) data (Cyr and Miller, 2023a). All these data combined were used in this study to construct a basin- fill thickness map and refine the understanding of the subsurface hydrogeology in the AFRL area. All the data generated during this study are available as USGS data releases (Groover and others, 2020; Ely and Fenton, 2022; Catchings and others, 2023; Cyr and Miller, 2023b; Cook and others, 2025). The location of the Air Force Research Laboratory and subareas at Edwards Air Force Base, Antelope Valley, California. 4 Hydrogeologic Conceptualization of the Arroyos and NEAFRL Subareas, Edwards AFB, Antelope Valley , 2017–21 Description of Study Area The Edwards AFB lies within the Antelope Valley in the western part of the Mojave Desert of southern California, about 65 miles (mi) northeast of Los Angeles (fig. 1). The Antelope Valley is bounded by the Tehachapi Mountains to the northwest and the San Gabriel Mountains to the southwest. The AFRL covers about 125 square miles (mi 2) in the eastern part of Edwards AFB, east of Rogers Dry Lake and south of the town of Boron. The most prominent geographical feature in the study area is Leuhman Ridge ( fig. 1), a free- standing set of northwest- trending rocky hills with a maximum elevation of 3,360 ft. About 0.6 mi north of Leuhman Ridge is a free- standing, unnamed, granitic hill referred to as the “mound” in this report (fig. 2). The western side of Leuhman Ridge slopes westward and downward toward Rogers Dry Lake. The subareas of the AFRL are the South AFRL, Arroyos, NEAFRL, and Mars Boulevard, which are based on geographic location and direction of regional groundwater flow ( fig. 1; Earth Tech, 2005). This study focuses on the two northernmost subareas: the Arroyos and NEAFRL subareas. The climate of the region is semiarid to arid, with low precipitation, hot summers, and cool winters (Dutcher and Warts, 1963). Temperatures in the Antelope Valley range from about 60 to 120 degrees Fahrenheit (°F) in the summer and from about 40 to 90 °F in the winter (California Department of Water Resources, 2022). Average annual precipitation at a long- term rain gage near the city of Lancaster (fig. 1) was about 7.4 inches (in.) per year for the period 1974–2016 (Western Regional Climatic Center, 2020). Purpose and Scope The purpose of this study was to evaluate the geologic structures and lithologic characteristics in the subsurface that may act as conduits or impediments to groundwater movement, improve the understanding of groundwater flow in the AFRL, and better define where groundwater transitions between basin- fill sediment and bedrock. This report uses the geologic mapping of Cyr and Miller (2023a), the methods and interpretations from surface and subsurface geophysical studies, and a basin- fill thickness map to conduct this work. Previous Studies Regional hydrogeologic studies of Antelope Valley have been published since the early 1900s, beginning with Johnson (1911) and Thompson (1929), who reported on the general character of the valley’s geology and hydrology. Johnson (1911) provided the first overview of water resources and hydrologic components in the Antelope Valley. Thompson (1929) provided an overview of the geography, geology, hydrology, and some biology of the western Mojave Desert region, which included most of the Antelope Valley. The surface geology of Antelope Valley was mapped and described by Dibblee (1957, 1958, 1959, 1960, 1967, 1981), Dutcher and Warts (1963), Ponti and Burke (1980), Ponti and others (1981), and Dibblee and Minch (2008). These geologic maps covered broad areas of the Antelope Valley. Only Dibblee (1957, 1967, 1960) provided generalized surface geologic maps of the AFRL and the immediate surrounding areas. To better describe the surface geology of the northern part of the AFRL area, Cyr and Miller (2023a) mapped the surface geology at a scale of 1:18,000. The results of this mapping eff ort are summarized in the “Geology” section. Previous gravity surveys of the Antelope Valley include those conducted by Mabey (1960), Morin (1992), Ward and others (1993), and Langenheim and others (2019). Mabey (1960) conducted a gravity survey of the Antelope Valley and provided insight into the general geologic structure of the region. Morin (1992) acquired gravity measurements near Edwards AFB to determine the depth to granitic bedrock in the area. Ward and others (1993) published a gravity study with a specific interest in Rogers Dry Lake and the surrounding area to investigate fissures that opened on the lakebed. Langenheim and others (2019) published a gravity study of the AFRL and the surrounding area, the results of which are used in this study. Previous surface geophysical work at Edwards AFB includes those of Resolution Resources, Inc. (1997); AECOM (2013a, 2014a); and Catchings and others (2023). Resolution Resources, Inc. (1997) and AECOM (2014a) collected seismic survey data in the AFRL. AECOM (2013a) presented electrical resistivity tomography (ERT) and time- domain electromagnetic (TEM) survey data in the AFRL. Catchings and others (2023) collected a seismic reflection imaging survey across the Spring Fault Zone in the NEAFRL. Londquist and others (1993) were the first to describe the hydrogeology of Edwards AFB, specifically investigating land subsidence that had been observed across the southern part of Edwards AFB since 1926. Sneed and Galloway (2000) modeled aquifer compaction and land subsidence specifically at Rogers Dry Lake to assess the potential adverse effects on the runways for landing aircraft, such as the Space Shuttle. To estimate the eff ects of continued pumping and injection strategies on water levels and land subsidence documented at Edwards AFB, Nishikawa and others (2001) developed a groundwater flow model for 1947–96 that covered the middle part of Edwards AFB but did not cover the AFRL to the east. A regional groundwater- flow model by Leighton and Phillips (2003), which was later refined and updated by Siade and others (2014), covered the Antelope Valley groundwater basin, which encompasses Rogers Dry Lake (fig. 1), but also did not include the AFRL area. AECOM (2014b) developed a conceptual site model and a groundwater- flow model of the NEAFRL subarea using hydrologic data from 1994 to 2008 to aid in the comparison of remediation techniques. The following sections describe the regional and local geologic settings, geologic and hydrogeologic units, and the faults that have been observed at the surface and inferred in the subsurface. Regional Setting The Antelope Valley is a structural depression bounded by the Garlock Fault Zone to the northwest, the San Andreas Fault Zone to the southwest, and the eastern California shear zone to the east (faults shown on fig. 1 are from the statewide geologic map of California by Jennings and others, 2010). These fault zones have extensively faulted the primarily granitoid plutonic rocks (Dibblee, 1967) that compose the bedrock of the Antelope Valley and have created large sediment- filled structural basins separated by areas of elevated bedrock (Sneed and Galloway, 2000). The large structural basins can be seen in gravity- derived depth- to- bedrock studies (Ward and others, 1993; Langenheim and others, 2019). A large structural low is evident in the northwest part of the AFRL, about 2.5 mi northwest of Leuhman Ridge (Langenheim and others, 2019). This structural low likely resulted either from stresses related to strike- slip motion along nearby faults or from early Miocene extension, the latter of which is similar to other small basins reported in the region (Langenheim and others, 2019). Local Setting The AFRL’s geology generally consists of crystalline bedrock overlain by unconsolidated sediment (fig. 2; Dibblee, 1960; Cyr and Miller, 2023a). The crystalline bedrock forms structural highs and lows (fig. 2), and as expected, the unconsolidated basin- fill sediment are thickest in the areas of bedrock lows. The crystalline bedrock is composed primarily of granite and granodiorite exposed in the Kramer Hills, Leuhman Ridge, and “mound,” with isolated small outcrops found in washes along the southern edge of the Kramer Hills and exposed at the bases of the arroyos (steep- sided gullys) draining the eastern side of Leuhman Ridge (fig. 2; Dibblee, 1960; Cyr and Miller, 2023a). Crystalline bedrock also is exposed in pediments to the south and east of Leuhman Ridge (Cyr and Miller, 2023a). Dibblee (1960) inferred that the crystalline bedrock in the AFRL is similar to the granitic intrusions of the Sierra Nevada granitic batholith, and therefore indicated these rocks are Late Jurassic or Early Cretaceous; the crystalline bedrock was questionably identified as Cretaceous by Cyr and Miller (2023a). In this report, the crystalline bedrock is shown on figure 2 as a single unit. For more detailed geologic mapping of the crystalline bedrock, refer to Dibblee (1960) and Cyr and Miller (2023a). Crystalline bedrock, as shown on figure 2 and described above, can be separated into competent bedrock and weathered bedrock (grus or saprolite). Competent bedrock is broadly defined here as crystalline rock that has undergone no (or minimal) physical alteration and is not chemically weathered. Competent bedrock includes structurally sound crystalline bedrock (rock that does not show substantial fracturing or weathering and has limited hydraulic conductivity) and fractured crystalline rock (rock that is broken or cracked in preferential orientations due to regional and local stresses). Fractured crystalline bedrock was identified extensively throughout the AFRL by Cyr and Miller (2023a). Moderate to high fracture density was observed on Leuhman Ridge, with the density increasing to the north in the pass between Leuhman Ridge and “mound.” Up to moderate fracturing was also identified in the exposed crystalline bedrock in the Kramer Hills. Cyr and Miller (2023a) did not separate the fractured crystalline bedrock into different units and instead used fracture density as evidence for Quaternary faulting. Fractured bedrock may transmit large quantities of groundwater and therefore be an important part of the groundwater system in the AFRL area. Weathered bedrock is crystalline bedrock that has undergone substantial physical or chemical weathering. Weathered bedrock was identified in the AFRL by Dibblee (1960), Cromwell and others (2020), and Cyr and Miller (2023a). Grus and other alluvial sediment associated with the weathering of exposed bedrock were observed by Dibblee (1960, 1967), although these materials were not mapped as a separate unit because the materials may be interlayered with other alluvial deposits and were not distinguishable in map view. A veneer of loose alluvial sediment consisting of weathered bedrock was mapped by Cyr and Miller (2023a; their unit Qpv) in the Kramer Hills and on the pediments south and east of Leuhman Ridge (unit Qwl; fig. 2). This weathered bedrock material is alluvial and generally less than about 6 ft thick (Cyr and Miller, 2023a). On the pediment east of Leuhman Ridge, Cyr and Miller (2023a) observed thicker layers of grus deposited on the pediment surface. Weathered bedrock material is present in the subsurface in many places based on reported intervals of grus and other material associated with weathering of crystalline rock in lithologic drillers’ logs (AECOM, 2013b, 2014a, b; Cromwell and others, 2020), and similar to exposed areas, likely is interlayered with alluvial deposits. The basin- fill sediment in the AFRL is composed of unconsolidated Quaternary (Pleistocene to Holocene) groundwater discharge deposits (Qigw), eolian deposits (Qe), and alluvial deposits (Qa) that unconformably overlie the bedrock, and are composed of fine- to coarse- grained sands, silts, and gravels (fig. 2; Dibblee, 1960; Cyr and Miller, 2023a). Also mapped are Holocene modified land (ml; fig. 2; Cyr and Miller, 2023a), where human disturbance made the landforms or deposits difficult to identify. In this report, the basin- fill units shown on figure 2 are simplified groupings of geologic units by Dibblee (1960) and Cyr and Miller (2023a). Geology 7 Pleistocene groundwater discharge (Qigw; fig. 2) are found along the western strands of the Spring Fault Zone near the northwest termination of the Kramer Hills (Cyr and Miller, 2023a). Eolian (windblown) deposits (Qe; fig. 2) are found along the base of Leuhman Ridge to the west and east of Rogers Dry Lake. These eolian deposits were mapped by Dibblee (1960, 1967) as windblown sand (their unit Qs) and Cyr and Miller (2023a) as intermediate age (middle to late Pleistocene) eolian sheet deposits, young (latest Pleistocene to Holocene) eolian sand sheet deposits, and young eolian dune deposits (their units Qies, Qyes, and Qyed, respectively). Alluvial deposits (Qa; fig. 2) are found east and west of Leuhman Ridge and southwest of Kramer Hills. These deposits were mapped by Dibblee (1960, 1967) as alluvial gravel and sand (their unit Qa) and by Cyr and Miller (2023a) as intermediate age alluvial fan deposits, young alluvial fan deposits, and young alluvial deposits with a substantial addition of eolian material (their units Qia, Qya, and Qyae, respectively). Hydrogeologic Units The geologic materials in the AFRL include structurally sound crystalline bedrock, fractured crystalline bedrock, weathered bedrock, and unconsolidated basin- fill sedimentary materials. In this report, these materials are generalized as three hydrogeologic units—bedrock, weathered bedrock, and basin- fill sediment. Following Cyr and Miller (2023a), bedrock includes structurally sound crystalline bedrock and fractured bedrock. Faults Quaternary faults are present throughout the Antelope Valley, which is bounded on the northwest by the Garlock Fault Zone and to the southwest by the San Andreas Fault Zone (fig. 1; Dibblee, 1960, 1967; Cyr and Miller, 2023a). Two high- angle, right- lateral, northwest- trending Quaternary faults are identified in the AFRL: (1) the Spring Fault Zone and (2) the Leuhman Fault (fig. 2; Dibblee, 1960; U.S. Geological Survey, and California Geological Survey, 2022; Cyr and Miller, 2023a). These two faults align with the general stresses of the Mojave Desert region, which are strike- slip in nature and broadly trend to the northwest (Cyr and Miller, 2023a). The Arroyos, Mound, Boron Gate, and Rich Faults (fig. 2) were newly identified by Cyr and Miller (2023a) and are northeast- southwest- trending faults, except for the Mound Fault, which is mostly east- west trending. Spring Fault Zone The Spring Fault Zone is a northwest- southeast- trending, oblique right- lateral fault at the southwestern edge of the Kramer Hills with several splays at its northwestern end (fig. 2; Cyr and Miller, 2023a). The fault zone was originally mapped by Dibblee (1960, 1967), who recognized a single fault strand along its mapped extent. Cyr and Miller (2023a) refined the mapped extent of the fault zone and identified at least five separate fault splays toward the northwest end of the fault. The splays of the Spring Fault from Cyr and Miller (2023a) are referred to in this report as the Spring Fault Zone (fig. 2). Dibblee (1960, 1967) estimated 150 ft of vertical displacement along the Spring Fault Zone based on scarp height. Cyr and Miller (2023a), using the gravity model of Langenheim and others (2019), estimated that the vertical offset could be as much as 400 ft. Cyr and Miller (2023a) estimated the lateral offset of the Spring Fault Zone to be a minimum of about 2 mi by examining the relationship of bedrock outcrops on either side of the fault splays. Cyr and Miller (2023a) also observed deformation in the Holocene alluvial sediments (Qya, Qia), indicating that the most recent activity along the fault was late Pleistocene to Holocene. Leuhman Fault The Leuhman Fault is a northwest- southeast- trending oblique strike- slip fault. In the AFRL, the fault is inferred to be concealed beneath basin- fill sediment (fig. 2; Dibblee, 1960, 1967; U.S. Geological Survey, and California Geological Survey, 2022). Cyr and Miller (2023a) reported little to no convincing surficial evidence for the existence of the Leuhman Fault in the AFRL. Geophysical surveys and groundwater- level data gathered as a part of this study indicate a subsurface structure nearly coincident with the inferred surface trace of the Leuhman Fault. These results are discussed in the “Surface Geophysics” and “Groundwater Flow and Movement” sections. Arroyos Fault, Mound Fault, Boron Gate Fault, and Rich Fault The Arroyos Fault, Mound Fault, Boron Gate Fault, and Rich Fault were identified and mapped by Cyr and Miller (2023a). These faults are northeast- southwest- trending faults, except for the Mound Fault, which is mostly east- west trending. Cyr and Miller (2023a) determined Quaternary age of faulting for the Boron Gate and Rich Faults using luminescence dating (Mahan and others, 2021) on sediment samples collected near each fault. No luminescence dating was done for the Arroyos or Mound Faults, although those faults are also likely Quaternary (Cyr and Miller, 2023a). Electrical Resistivity Tomography Seven ERT surveys (table 1) were completed in this study in June 2018 (Groover and others, 2020) and in September 2020 (Ely and Fenton, 2022). The 2018 ERT surveys were collected along four transects—three in the Arroyos subarea and one in the NEAFRL subarea (fig. 3; Groover and others, 2020). The 2020 ERT surveys were collected along two transects in the NEAFRL subarea near the Leuhman and Spring Faults (fig. 3; Ely and Fenton, 2022). In 2020, two surveys were collected along transect AFRL7E (table 1). Electrical resistivity tomography (also known as direct- current resistivity) is a direct- current geophysical method used to estimate the subsurface distribution of the electrical resistivity of a material, measured in ohm- meters (ohm- m). The method assumes that the measured electric potential (voltage) near current- carrying electrodes is affected by the electrical resistivity of the underlying material. ERT is a popular method for investigating subsurface materials because it is based on simple physical principles and because it allows for efficient data acquisition (Zohdy and others, 1974; Dahlin and Zhou, 2004). The ERT instrumentation and data collection design are highly configurable to resolve different types of subsurface features. The method also is generally resistant to anthropogenic noise. However, ERT data collection is labor and time intensive. To collect ERT data, a control unit injects an electric current into the ground through metallic stakes at two transmitting electrodes. The electric potential field is measured at nearby electrodes that act as receivers. Modern ERT systems use cables containing multiple electrodes that are automatically switched between transmitting and receiving in configurations known as arrays. Apparent resistivities are calculated based on the configuration of the geometry between the transmitting and receiving electrodes, the amount of current injected at the current electrodes, and the measured potentials at the receiver electrodes. A representation of the true subsurface resistivity is obtained through inverse modeling, which is the application of an iterative software program to compute the resistivity of an equivalent non- uniform earth material (Teeple and others, 2009). For two- dimensional (2D) arrays, information about lateral- resistivity variability in the subsurface is gained as the transmitting and receiving electrodes are translated across the array. Information about greater depths is obtained by increasing the separation between the current and potential electrodes. Details about the practical aspects of resistivity surveying techniques are described by Zohdy and others (1974) and Telford and others (1990), whereas the inverse modeling process is described in detail by Loke (2004), and a comparison of array types is described and compared by Dahlin and Zhou (2004). Descriptions of specific ERT field data collection methodology and instrumentation for this study are in the following USGS data releases: Groover and others (2020) and Ely and Fenton (2022). The ERT data were collected with three array types: (1) inverse- Schlumberger array, (2) dipole- dipole array, and (3) strong- gradient array. The inverse- Schlumberger array uses pairs of receiver electrodes placed symmetrically on either side of a pair of transmitting electrodes, providing a balance between lateral and depth resolution (Minsley and others, 2010). The dipole- dipole array uses pairs of receiver electrodes offset from a pair of transmitting electrodes, which yields a high- precision dataset, particularly of vertical structures, but can show lower signal- to- noise ratios (Dahlin and Zhou, 2004; Binley and Kemna, 2005). The strong- gradient array records data at pairs of receiver electrodes within a pair of transmitting electrodes and provides more complete spatial coverage and a high signal- to- noise ratio with increased acquisition efficiency (Dahlin and Zhou, 2004, 2006; Advanced Geosciences, Inc., 2009). Data from two of the dipole- dipole arrays were processed using a complex command file that combined the dipole- dipole and strong- gradient array types. The different methods produce slightly different results, complicating direct comparison of profiles collected using different array types. However, within the scope of this study, the differences were within the error of analysis. Time- Domain Electromagnetic Surveys Time- domain electromagnetic (TEM) surveys, AFRL2T, AFRL4T, and AFRL5T, were collected in the Arroyos subarea at 22 points along companion ERT survey transects (fig. 3). These data were collected between May and October 2018 (Groover and others, 2020). In the TEM method, a transmitter is used to inject electrical current into a square transmitter loop of insulated wire laid on the ground, and a receiver is used to measure the current induced in the subsurface. The transmitter current has discrete on- and- off periods, and termination of the current flow, known as the ramp time, occurs over several microseconds. During the ramp time, the time- varying nature of the primary magnetic field creates a secondary magnetic field in the subsurface below the loop that, in accordance with Faraday’s law, generally mirrors the transmitter loop (Christiansen and others, 2006). The secondary field immediately begins to decay, generating secondary eddy currents that propagate downward and outward into the subsurface (Williams and others, 2021). Measurements of the secondary eddy currents are made during the time- off period by one or more receivers in the center of the transmitter loop. The slope of the decay curve is controlled by the bulk resistivity of the subsurface, which includes the resistivity of subsurface sediment and rock units, and the fluids contained therein (Stewart and Gay, 1986; McNeill, 1994; Auken and others, 2008). During transmitter off time, a receiver at the center of the transmitter loop measures the secondary magnetic field as a function of time. Decaying voltage measurements at the receiver are converted to apparent resistivity, which can be inverted to recover the depth- dependent vertical resistivity structure of the Earth below the receiver location. The general procedure for collecting the TEM data includes the following steps and is discussed in detail in a USGS data release by Groover and others (2020), which describes the specific TEM field data collection methodology, instrumentation, measurement cycles, processing, and resistivity inversions. During the data collection process, care was taken to maximize distance from electrical power lines and metal objects (such as pipelines, military infrastructure or equipment, and other debris) within or near the array. Metal objects within and near the array can adversely affect the survey by causing induction effects that limit the penetration of electrical current into the subsurface. Proximity to buried infrastructure, power, or telephone lines can induce noise in the survey. Attempts were made to position the TEM data collection locations along the same transect as the ERT surveys, but proximity to buried infrastructure ultimately guided the TEM survey locations. This resulted in surveys being collected as close to the originally planned transects as possible. A depth of investigation (DOI) calculation was completed for each sounding using methods described by Christiansen and Auken (2012). The DOI represents the depth to which geophysical instruments are sensitive to the physical properties of the Earth. Thus, any results interpreted at depths below the DOI (table 2) should be considered unreliable. The DOI can vary depending on the material being measured, the geophysical method being used, and the instrument setup. Horizontal- to- Vertical Spectral Ratio Surveys Passive seismic data were collected in the Arroyos subarea using the HVSR method (Groover and others, 2020) at 42 stations (fig. 3) between May and September 2018 to help determine the depth to bedrock. The HVSR method is a passive seismic technique that uses a three- component seismometer to measure the vertical and horizontal components of ambient seismic noise. A resonance frequency (f0) is induced in basin- fill sediment when there is a substantial contrast (greater than 2:1) in shear- wave acoustic impedance between the basin- fill sediment overburden and the bedrock. The f0 is determined from analysis of the spectral ratio of the horizontal and vertical components of the seismic data. The thickness of the overburden can be related to the f0. In general, lower f0 relates to thicker sediments, and higher f0 relates to relatively thinner overburden. For seismic surveys, the ground is stressed by an explosion or a hammer blow that generates three fundamental types of elastic waves: primary/push- pull (P) waves, secondary/shear (S) waves, and surface waves. The P- and S- waves propagate through the body of the Earth; the surface waves can exist only close to the free surface. Seismic imaging can evaluate several characteristics of the subsurface over longer linear distances and deeper depths than the other surface geophysical techniques described in this report. The seismic imaging survey was about 10,000 ft long and produced viable subsurface imaging down to a depth of more than 1,000 ft bls. Geologic materials, faults, and likely depth to groundwater were estimated from analysis of P- wave and S- wave velocities over a depth of 0 ft to about 1,310 ft bls. The seismic imaging results are briefly discussed in the “Surface Geophysics Results” section. For more details, refer to Catchings and others (2023). Surface Geophysics Results This section describes the results of each surface geophysical survey collected as part of this study. The surface geophysical surveys provide local estimates of subsurface hydrogeology of the study area. Most of the surveys resolve the depth and geometry of bedrock and the contact between bedrock and overlying hydrogeologic units. Some surveys resolve the location and geometry of faults, and some surveys indicate the presence of saturated and unsaturated materials. Individual survey results are not, on their own, sufficient to resolve the difference between weathered bedrock and basin- fill sediment. Multiple survey types collected along the same transects, however, can be used to infer the difference between weathered bedrock and basin- fill sediment when evaluated with borehole lithology data and geologic maps (refer to the “Hydrogeologic Characterization” section). Time- Domain Electromagnetic Surveys The processed TEM data from individual soundings were inverted without constraints to produce 20- layer smooth models of subsurface resistivity. The 20- layer smooth models use multiple layers with fixed thicknesses that change gradually, thereby “smoothing” the transition from layer to layer. These lines connect areas containing borehole lithology information (Cromwell and others, 2020) and measurements of the depth- to- water to provide information about the basin- fill sediment to bedrock transition zone. In general, the cool colors shown in the TEM survey results indicate low- resistivity values that are associated with fine- grained earth materials, and the warm colors indicate high- resistivity values that are generally associated with coarse- grained earth materials. Information about the individual sounding locations and DOI is presented in table 2. In TEM transect AFRL2T (fig. 11), the top of bedrock ranges from about 100 ft bls in the southwestern part of the transect to about 300 ft bls in the northeastern part of the transect. As bedrock deepens, the basin- fill sediment decreases in resistivity, indicating that fine- grained sediments compose the basin- fill sediment layer in this area. Where AFRL2T and AFRL2E coincide (fig. 3), the two surveys show the same trend in bedrock depth and slope (figs. 5, 11).
