The city also maintains wells to withdraw groundwater, particularly in times of drought and reduced surface-water supply, and to recharge groundwater when excess surface water is available. As of 2023, withdrawals from the aquifer in the northeastern part of the city are a small volume of water, less than 3,000 acre-feet in most years. Each year a similar volume of water is recharged through injection wells. The withdrawal wells are permitted to produce in total more than 20,000 acre-feet per year; increased pumping could lead to future groundwater-storage declines. To better understand groundwater-storage change in the north Phoenix aquifer, a repeat microgravity monitoring network was established in 2020. Measurements of changes in Earth’s gravity provide a direct, non-invasive measurement of subsurface mass change. Groundwater-storage changes were small during the 2020–23 study period, consistent with the relatively small volumes of pumping and recharge. Groundwater levels measured in monitoring wells were stable or increased slightly during this period, although the number of monitoring wells within the area of the gravity monitoring network is sparse. In total, about 15,000 acre-feet of water were pumped and 31,000 acre-feet recharged through injection in the north Phoenix aquifer during the 2020–22 period. Within the monitored area, groundwater storage increased by a small amount, about 1,000 acre-feet, in 2020, and decreased by a larger amount, about 6,000 acre-feet, each year in 2021 and 2022. Storage decreased at 89 of 102 stations from 2020 to 2023. Groundwater-storage decreases greater than the volume of net pumping indicate down-gradient subflow from the aquifer is greater than recharge plus incoming subflow, drying of the unsaturated zone resulting from decreased land- surface recharge, or both. At present (2023), the aquifer appears able to store and supply the relatively small amounts of water needed without excessive drawdown or harmful effects, such as land subsidence and surface inundation. If pumping or recharge increases in the future, the established repeat microgravity network is well suited to capture the spatial extent and magnitude of groundwater-storage changes in the aquifer. Introduction Water sources for the city of Phoenix, Arizona, are surface water from the Salt, Verde, and Colorado Rivers; groundwater from basin-fill deposits, infiltrated streamflow, and managed aquifer recharge; and reclaimed water (effluent). The city lies within the Salt River Valley, a desert region home to more than 4 million people (U.S. Census Bureau, 2023) with a large and diverse economy (fig. 1). Cities in the Salt River Valley, which include Phoenix and 11 other cities of more than 75,000 inhabitants and several smaller towns and cities, have diverse water-use histories, water rights, and different groundwater, surface water, and reclaimed water-supply portfolios. They all are within the Phoenix Active Management Area (fig. 1), designated by the Arizona Department of Water Resources (ADWR) as an area with increased restrictions on groundwater pumping to achieve the goal of sustainable groundwater management (ADWR, 2024b). The city of Phoenix water utility (Phoenix Water Services) is the largest water utility in the State, providing water to more than 1,700,000 customers in a 520 square mile service area (City of Phoenix, 2021). For the 2010–19 period, about 1,300,000 acre-feet of water were delivered annually, but only about 2 percent was groundwater, 26,000 acre-feet per year on average, for this period (ADWR, 2023a). To augment and strengthen the city’s groundwater portfolio, in 2018 the city began drilling and installing additional artificial storage and recovery (ASR) wells for managed aquifer recharge in the northeast part of the city. In years with abundant surface-water supply, particularly from the Colorado River, these ASR wells are intended to inject (under gravity flow) excess water into the north Phoenix aquifer. In drier years, when Colorado River water or other supplies may be reduced, this stored water can be pumped and delivered to customers. As of 2023, the effort to construct and equip ASR wells is ongoing. Groundwater monitoring in the study area includes metering the volume of water at each well where water is injected or pumped and depth-to-groundwater measurements at about 10 wells carried out by the ADWR. Pumping occurs at only a few high-capacity wells and more widely distributed residential wells are scarce. Therefore, groundwater pumped from any individual municipal well may affect groundwater storage over a large area. To better understand groundwater-storage change in the north Phoenix aquifer, a repeat microgravity network comprising about 105 stations was established (fig. 2). Gravity surveys of this network were done annually in the winters of 2020, 2021, 2022, and 2023. Each survey represents a snapshot of Earth’s gravity field at the time they were taken, and the change in gravity can be calculated between any two snapshots. Repeat microgravity provides a direct, non-invasive measurement of subsurface mass change. In desert alluvial basins, typically the only large source of mass change is from changes in aquifer storage or in soil- water content; therefore, the gravity changes can be interpreted directly as water-storage changes using a linear relation known as the horizontal infinite slab approximation. Purpose and Scope The purpose of this report is to present the interpretation of repeat microgravity and groundwater-level data as they relate to groundwater-storage change in the north Phoenix aquifer. Data were collected at about 105 stations during annual surveys from 2020 through 2023 to measure changes in groundwater storage resulting from pumping and managed aquifer recharge in the aquifer. A subset of stations, approximately half, were observed in the first year. Microgravity and groundwater-level data are presented in context with groundwater pumping and recharge data reported by pumping entities to the ADWR. Today, the town of Paradise Valley occupies a relatively small (15.4 square miles) area at the far south end of the geographic Paradise Valley, which stretches roughly 25 miles north to south, from near Black Mountain and other ranges near the town of Cave Creek in the north, to the Salt River in the south, and 10 miles east to west between the McDowell Mountains and the Phoenix Mountains (fig. 2). The repeat microgravity network and the area where the city of Phoenix primarily pumps groundwater is in the northern part of Paradise Valley, an area bounded roughly by Cave Creek Road to the west, 64th Street to the east, Carefree Highway to the north, and the Loop 101 Freeway to the south. The study area lies within the semiarid Sonoran Desert. The 30-year (1991–2020) average annual rainfall for the study area was 10.2 inches (PRISM Climate Group, 2023). Annual rainfall in 2020, 2021, and 2022 at a rainfall gage near the Scottsdale Airport (about 1 mile southeast of the intersection of Bell Road and Scottsdale Road, fig. 2) was 2.96 inches, 9.48 inches, and 12.91 inches, respectively (National Weather Service, 2023). The maximum average monthly high temperature is in July, 102 degrees Fahrenheit (°F), and the minimum monthly low temperature is in December, 42 °F. Temperatures routinely exceed 110 °F in summer. The city of Phoenix is the primary water provider in the study area (fig. 2). The other significant water provider in the area is the city of Scottsdale, which borders Phoenix to the east and pumps and recharges in the same aquifer. The towns of Cave Creek and Carefree operate water utilities, including the Desert Hills Water Company, north of the study area, but deliver primarily Colorado River water by the Central Arizona Project (CAP) (CAP, 2024) and pump a smaller amount (less than 1,000 acre-feet per year) of groundwater (ADWR, 2024a). Previous Studies An early report by Meinzer and Ellis (1915) on the potential groundwater resources in Paradise Valley characterized depth to water and water quality. Agriculture and residential development in the study area at that time was minimal, although more than 50 new wells were noted south of the Old Verde Canal, which followed the alignment of the CAP canal in the southern part of the study area (fig. 2). Cave Creek was noted as ephemeral (“normally dry”), except for a short reach at Cave Creek Station (near the present- day town of Cave Creek, north of the study area), as it was by Lee (1905) 10 years earlier. Depth to water in the southernmost part of the study area, near the Old Verde/CAP Canals, was 300 ft or less. No data were available north of there, but it was noted depth to water would increase in the north as the land-surface elevation increased. Runoff from Cave Creek and the McDowell Mountains was noted as the primary source of recharge to the aquifer, with a particular focus on gravelly, buried former channels of Cave Creek as favorable pathways for subsurface flow. A 1947 report on the groundwater resources of Paradise Valley (McDonald and others, 1947) showed just one groundwater well in the central part of the study area (near Dynamite Road and 56th Street; depth to water in 1946 was 626 ft); most wells at that time were south of the study area near Bell Road and along Scottsdale Road. Groundwater-level drawdown (change from initial conditions at a well) was as much as 40 ft in the southern part of Paradise Valley, but near zero further north (in the southern part of the present study area; no data were available farther north). Because of large depths to water in much of Paradise Valley, groundwater use for irrigation was limited to small areas with the shallowest water tables (McDonald and others, 1947). Later, Arteaga and others (1968) did a comprehensive investigation on groundwater conditions in Paradise Valley, but most of their data and analyses focus on the southern part of the valley, south of the study area. Three informal alluvial units (upper, middle, and lower) forming a single aquifer system were identified; in northern Paradise Valley, wells were less productive than to the south because they did not penetrate the more permeable lower alluvium, and the upper alluvium was dry. Arteaga and others (1968) noted that in the southern part of Paradise Valley and near Scottsdale pumping greatly exceeded recharge and cones of depression had formed. In northern Paradise Valley (the study area), they noted groundwater from alluvium was the main source of water, and 2 public supply, 1 irrigation, and about 30 domestic wells were in the area. In total, less than 10,000 acre-feet were pumped in northern Paradise Valley from 1946 through 1965 and change in groundwater level from 1961 to 1966 was almost zero, although declines of 25 to 50 ft were observed in central and southern Paradise Valley. Depth to bedrock (that is, the thickness of alluvial fill) throughout Paradise Valley was estimated by Lausten (1974) from 371 gravity stations and borehole logs. Low-density basin fill causes a gravity “low,” the magnitude of which is proportional to the thickness of fill. This depth-to-bedrock application of gravity data differs from the repeat microgravity surveys presented in this report to measure change in groundwater storage. It is an interpretation of the large-scale density features of a single “snapshot” of the gravity field and does not consider any change in gravity. Laney and Hahn (1978) published a series of maps showing the distribution of sedimentary facies, groundwater-level contours, and aquifer transmissivity in the east Salt River Valley, including the southern part of Paradise Valley. The northernmost extent of their analysis barely overlaps with the southern part of the study area, but their work provides helpful descriptions of the alluvial units where applicable. Geology The study area is primarily an alluvial fan setting with Tertiary to Quaternary coarse-grained granitic sediment derived from mountains to the north, the McDowell Mountains to the east, and the Phoenix Mountains to the west and south. These Methods 5 bounding mountain ranges are primarily Proterozoic igneous and metamorphic rocks and are not considered part of the groundwater flow system (ADWR, 2023b). The alluvial basin deepens from north to south through the study area, reaching a maximum thickness of 12,000 ft or more around Union Hills Road and Tatum Boulevard (Lausten, 1974). Farther north, along Pinnacle Peak Road, the maximum depth is about 5,000 ft; along Lone Mountain Road, it is about 2,500 ft; and along Carefree Highway, it is about 500 ft. Boreholes penetrating the entire alluvial fill are sparse, but an exploration well near Pinnacle Peak and Tatum Roads apparently met basement material at 5,150 ft below land surface (Laney and Hahn, 1978). The alluvium is generally divided into informal lower, middle, and upper units (Arteaga and others, 1968), with the middle alluvium unit being generally the finest grained. The lower alluvium unit is generally coarse grained, well cemented, and interbedded with basaltic and andesitic lava flows (Arteaga and others, 1968). The middle alluvium unit includes playa, alluvial fan, and fluvial deposits and is estimated to be greater than 70 percent clay and silt south of the study area but coarsens considerably to more than 70 percent sand and gravel in the southernmost part of the study area (Laney and Hahn, 1978). In the southern part of Paradise Valley, it may be a source of naturally occurring nitrate (Silver and Fielden, 1980) and hexavalent chromium in groundwater (Robertson, 1975). The upper alluvium unit is in the upper few hundred feet of the subsurface and is dry in the study area (Arteaga and others, 1968). Hydrology The major surface-water feature in the study area is Cave Creek, an ephemeral wash (channel) originating in the central Arizona highlands north of Paradise Valley. An additional ephemeral wash, Apache Wash, parallels Cave Creek to the west. The drainage area of Cave Creek and Apache Wash at Cave Buttes Dam in the southwest part of the study area is approximately 195 square miles (U.S. Geological Survey, 2023). Cave Buttes Dam partially impounds ephemeral flows during periods of runoff, but impounded water drains quickly, and there is no significant surface reservoir most of the year. Other surface-water features in the study area are ephemeral washes that flow northeast to southwest, but only in response to rainfall. Although no direct streamgaging measurements are available for these washes, they can carry substantial flows occasionally (once every few years). The CAP canal delivers water from the Colorado River to the Phoenix and Tucson metropolitan areas. The concrete-lined canal traverses the southern part of the study area from west to east following a similar alignment to the Old Verde Canal, a failed effort in the early 1900s to transport water east to west from the Verde River to the channel of Cave Creek, the goal of which was to establish irrigated agriculture in the Paradise Valley (Arteaga and others, 1968). Because the CAP is concrete lined through the study area, seepage and direct recharge from the canal is assumed to be minimal. Groundwater in the study area is generally at depths greater than 500 ft below land surface—the greatest depths to groundwater in the entire Salt River Valley (ADWR, 2023b). There is little discussion in the historical references about confining or artesian conditions in the aquifer. McDonald and others (1947, p. 11) “found no structural features in the valley or in the bordering mountains that would indicate the presence of artesian water in Paradise Valley.” Arteaga and others (1968) mention a semiconfining layer in the southern part of Paradise Valley, south of the study area; however, they provide no discussion about this feature. Larson and Péwé (1986) describe unconfined conditions in their study area bordered by Bell Road in the north (south of the repeat microgravity network), but “near the northern boundary of the study area,” a clay layer 200 ft thick creates confining conditions. The presence of unconfined or confined conditions is significant for this study because it determines the relation between storage (measured by gravity) and groundwater-level change (measured in monitoring wells) according to equation 2. Confined conditions can also indicate a potential for Earth fissuring. Earth fissuring was observed at least since the early 1980s in the southern part of Paradise Valley, about 3 miles south of the study area (Larson and Péwé, 1986). Based on these references, well logs in the study area, and alluvial sediments that coarsen to the north in Paradise Valley, closer to the mountain fronts, unconfined aquifer conditions are inferred throughout the study area. Several underground storage facilities, in the form of injection and vadose zone wells (the former screened at or below the water table, the latter above the water table), operate in and near the study area. Phoenix is permitted for 11 such wells, although only three operated during the study period (ADWR, 2024a). These wells are screened (open to the aquifer) at depths about 100 ft or more below the water table. Scottsdale operates three underground storage facilities near the study area: the Water Campus, West World Golf Course, and the North Scottsdale Aquifer Storage & Recovery Project (ADWR, 2024a). Each of these recharge water through vadose zone recharge wells. Error in the horizontal infinite slab approximation is less than 10 percent if the water table is approximately flat to a distance (radius) equal to 10 times the depth to water (Pool and Eychaner, 1995). The change in storage is related to the change in groundwater level (for a single aquifer) as: ΔGWL = ΔStorage/ne (2) where ΔGWL is the change in groundwater level measured in a well, ΔStorage is the change in thickness of free-standing water, and ne is the aquifer effective porosity (approximately equal to specific yield in an unconfined aquifer). The total volume of storage change between surveys was calculated by interpolating values within a polygon encompassing the repeat gravity network. This method converts the one-dimensional storage change at each measurement location to a three-dimensional volume of water and is equivalent to determining an anomalous mass from Gauss’ law (Blakely, 1995; Pool and Anderson, 2007). Interpolation was done by kriging, a method that estimates the semi-variogram (the variance-distance relation) of the observed data and predicts a value (in this case, change in gravity between surveys) at all locations on a grid. This study used a spherical semi-variogram model, consistent with previous gravity kriging efforts (for example, Shamsipour and others, 2010; Arfaoui and Inoubli, 2013; Kennedy and Bell, 2023). The semi-variogram nugget, representing measurement error, was set to 0.28 ft of water. This nugget estimate represents the average error from each survey (about 2.5 µGal) summed in quadrature (because the quantity being kriged is the change in gravity between surveys, not the gravity value for each survey) and converted to feet of water using equation 1. The semi-variogram standard deviation, which represents interpolation error, was also set to 0.28 inches of water; total variance at each interpolation location is the nugget plus additional variance as a function of the location’s distance from observation locations. The semi-variogram range, or distance at which spatial correlation approaches zero, was set to 6,000 ft or about 10 times the average depth-to-water in the study area. This distance represents the region over which individual gravity measurements are sensitive to subsurface mass change (Blakely, 1995). Prediction of the interpolated surface was done by universal kriging, which accounts for a linear trend in the interpolated surface. An advantage of the kriging method for interpolation is that it also produces an estimate of the variance, or uncertainty, associated with the interpolated values. The absolute-gravity meter (Micro-g Lacoste, Inc. A–10, serial number 008, Lafayette, Colo.) measures the acceleration of a free-falling mass and provides reference (“base station”) values for the relative surveys. Relative-gravity meters (ZLS Burris meters, serial numbers B–44, B–108, and E4D65, ZLS Corporation, Austin, Texas) measure the difference in gravity between two locations. Relative- and absolute-gravity meters are accurate to about 3–8 microGal (µGal; Crossley, 2013); 1 µGal is 1×10−8 meter per second squared and equivalent to about 2.38 centimeters of water using the horizontal-infinite slab approximation (eq. 1). Absolute-gravity measurements and relative-gravity differences were combined in a process known as least-squares network adjustment using GSadjust software (Kennedy, 2020). In 2021, a GNSS survey was done using a combination of static and real-time kinematic methods. Stations were positioned in the North American Datum of 1983 (2011) epoch 2010.00 datum using the Online Positioning User Service (National Geodetic Survey, 2023; Kennedy 2024). Corrections to gravity data focus on accounting for the time-varying changes in the gravity field not related to water- mass change, including land-surface elevation change, Earth tides, ocean loading, and atmospheric pressure change (Kennedy and others, 2021). To evaluate land-surface elevation change, interferometric synthetic aperture radar (InSAR) data were processed by ADWR (ADWR, 2023c). Future GNSS surveys can be compared with the 2021 survey to further verify land-surface elevation change (or lack thereof). Earth tide and ocean loading corrections were done using standard models included with data collection software. The gravitational effect of atmospheric pressure change on absolute-gravity measurements is accounted for by measuring air pressure at the time of each measurement and applying a −0.30 µGal/millibar admittance (correction) factor. Relative-gravity data were not corrected for air pressure because only relative-gravity differences between stations observed close together in time are considered in the least-squares network adjustment, assuming that (large) air pressure changes occur more slowly than data are collected. Although natural subflow and recharge are difficult or impossible to quantify, the upgradient recharge zone northeast of the study area is fairly small and includes a large expanse of Proterozoic granite at the land surface, which may limit recharge owing to potentially low porosity. Therefore, fluctuations in recharge and subflow in response to precipitation forcing may be relatively quick within that recharge zone, meaning that drought conditions since about 2000 (Leeper and others, 2022) may contribute toward reducing recharge and subflow. Another cause of storage declines greater than that caused by net pumping may be drying of the unsaturated zone. This process—potentially caused by lower-than-average precipitation since about the year 2000 (Leeper and others, 2022)—may produce a change (decrease) in gravity without a corresponding change in groundwater level. Ultimately, a drying unsaturated zone will eventually result in a reduction in recharge to the aquifer and declining groundwater levels, but the thick unsaturated zone in the study area, over 500 ft in most areas, may make the reduction in recharge and declining groundwater levels from atmospheric forcing and precipitation less noticeable in the short term (Dickinson and Ferré, 2018). Summary A repeat microgravity network was established in 2020 for the purpose of quantifying aquifer storage change in the north Phoenix aquifer. Although relatively small volumes of groundwater are pumped from the aquifer as of 2023, the city has developed the well infrastructure and permitted capacity to increase pumping to more than 20,000 acre-feet per year. Our analysis demonstrates that the repeat microgravity network is well suited to capture the effects of potential increased pumping and (or) changes in aquifer storage resulting from managed aquifer recharge. The repeat microgravity network comprises 108 stations established in 2020 and 2021, bounded roughly by Carefree Highway to the north, the Loop 101 freeway to the south, Cave Creek Road to the west, and 64th Street to the east. Stations were established on existing concrete surfaces, such as sidewalks. Gravity surveys were done using a combination of absolute- and relative-gravity meters. A Global Navigation Satellite System survey was done in 2021 to locate the stations and to allow stations to be evaluated for vertical movement in the future; vertical movement causes gravity changes independent of changes caused by change in subsurface mass (that is, groundwater-storage change). Satellite imagery (interferometric synthetic aperture radar [InSAR]) indicates minimal elevation change during the study period. Gravity changes were generally small during the study period, commensurate with the relatively small volume of groundwater pumping and injection in and near the study area. From 2020 through 2022, groundwater storage changed on average by −0.21 feet per year within the study area, equivalent to −4,130±260 acre-feet over a 19,750-acre polygon encompassing the study area. This amount is less than the net 15,740 acre-feet difference between injection and pumping during this period. However, most of the injection is at one location: 22,158 acre-feet at the Scottsdale Water Campus during the study period. This relatively large amount appears to have little or no effect on storage change within the area of the repeat microgravity network. Because groundwater pumping and recharge in the basin is metered and reported—that is, no large, unknown sources withdraw or inject water—this suggests groundwater subflow out to adjacent basins is greater than incoming subflow, drying of the unsaturated zone, or both. During the study period and the past two decades, widespread groundwater-level declines have largely leveled off or even rebounded as groundwater pumping was reduced and managed artificial recharge increased. Nonetheless, groundwater- flow model simulations indicate groundwater-storage losses will continue over the next 100 years in the north Paradise Valley Basin, including the study area (ADWR, 2023b). Storage losses are driven by pumping in the Salt River Valley as a whole, which can exceed 800,000 acre-feet per year (ADWR, 2023b). To observe these potential storage changes, barring site disturbance, repeat gravity surveys can be done at any time in the future. These measurements of gravity change are possible because the surveys are tied to the absolute datum of Earth’s gravity field using absolute-gravity measurements rather than depending on “bedrock” stations for reference (that is, unchanging) values for relative-gravity-only surveys. One limitation of the data in this report is that repeat microgravity and groundwater-level measurements were only done once per year and reported volumes of pumping and injection are only available as cumulative volumes for each calendar year. More frequent gravity monitoring and reporting of pumping/injections could help identify seasonal patterns. Often, water demand, and therefore groundwater pumping, is highest in summer months, but the distribution of pumping each year within the study area is unknown. The reversal of groundwater declines in the study area in Paradise Valley can be seen as a positive result from sustainable groundwater management. Continued monitoring of the groundwater resource can support understanding of if this effect is short lived or sustainable; a previous microgravity investigation in Albuquerque tracked a multiyear increase in groundwater storage following surface-water importation, but this storage increase ceased when the surface-water supply decreased and groundwater References Cited 17 pumping resumed (Kennedy and Bell, 2023). Continued groundwater-level measurements (perhaps in an expanded monitoring-well network, with wells equipped with pressure transducers) beneficially augmented with continued repeat microgravity monitoring can help capture future groundwater- storage changes, especially if pumping and (or) injection increase.
