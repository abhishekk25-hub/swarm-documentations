Izuka, and Kolja Rotzoll Abstract The Waihe‘e- Kahaluʻu watershed and surrounding areas lie in a dike- impounded groundwater setting of the Ko‘olau Range, O‘ahu, Hawai‘i, where groundwater withdrawal from wells and tunnels have resulted in reductions in streamflow. Resource managers and surface- water users seek information that can be used to balance the needs of competing uses of groundwater and streamflow in the watershed. This study assesses the effects of four groundwater developments in the Waihe‘e- Kahalu‘u watershed—Kahaluu Tunnel, Waihee Tunnel, Kahaluu Well, and Waihee Incline Wells— on Kahalu‘u and Waihe‘e Streams. Analyses of historical streamflow, withdrawal, and rainfall data indicate that base flow (the component of streamflow that is supplied from groundwater discharge) in Kahalu‘u and Waihe‘e Streams decreased in response to withdrawals from wells and tunnels within and outside the watershed. Groundwater- model simulations indicate that shutdown of selected tunnels and wells in the Waihe‘e- Kahaluʻu watershed will result in increases of groundwater discharge, but not all of the increase will occur in streams within the watershed; increases will also occur in streams outside the basin, the ocean, and tunnels other than those that were shut down. The analysis of historical data and model simulations is consistent in showing that the effects of withdrawal changes on streams are not constrained by the topographic boundaries of watersheds. Stream base flows in the Waihe‘e- Kahalu‘u watershed are subject to a complex interaction of multiple withdrawals from wells and tunnels installed at various times within and outside the watershed. The correspondence between withdrawals and base- flow changes is consistent with the conceptual model and understanding of the connection between groundwater and streamflow in the dike- impounded groundwater setting of the Waihe‘e- Kahalu‘u watershed and the Ko‘olau Range. Introduction The Waihe’e- Kahaluʻu watershed is in the Koʻolau Range on the island of O‘ahu, Hawai‘i (fig. 1). The watershed and surrounding areas lie in a dike- impounded groundwater setting, where groundwater is stored in compartments formed by the intrusion of near- vertical, low- permeability, sheet- like volcanic dikes into near- horizontal, high- permeability lava flows (fig. 2; Takasaki and Mink, 1985; Izuka and others, 2018). Groundwater accumulates to high altitudes in the compartments and discharges to streams and springs where erosion has breached the compartments. Groundwater withdrawal from the dike- impounded groundwater setting can result in reductions in natural groundwater discharge to streams and springs. Groundwater is withdrawn for domestic and agricultural use from tunnels (the Waihee and Kahaluu Tunnels) and several wells in the Waiheʻe- Kahaluʻu watershed (fig. 1). Groundwater withdrawals have resulted in the reduction of stream base flow (Hirashima, 1962, 1963, 1971; Takasaki and others, 1969). Surface water is diverted from Waiheʻe and Kahaluʻu Streams to support taro and other agriculture production. Persistent base flow from groundwater discharge also provides habitats for aquatic biota, including a migratory path for diadromous (species that migrate between saltwater and freshwater) fish (Pacific Islands Fish and Wildlife Office, 2015). Under a 1982 Hawai‘i Supreme Court order, Reppun v. Board of Water Supply, 2.7 million gallons per day (Mgal/d; 1.75 cubic feet per second [ft3/s]) of streamflow must be maintained at the U.S. Geological Survey (USGS) streamgage 16284200 on Waiheʻe Stream (U.S. Geological Survey, 2022; fig. 1) for traditional, customary, and riparian uses in the downstream areas (Reppun v. Board of Water Supply, 1982). The State of Hawai‘i Commission on Water Resource Management (CWRM) is seeking to develop Interim Instream-Flow Standards (IIFS) for Waiheʻe and Kahaluʻu Streams. The IIFS establishes minimum streamflow while balancing the needs for competing uses, including instream uses such as ecological, cultural, and aesthetic uses, with non- instream uses, including stream diversions and groundwater withdrawals that can affect stream base flow. Purpose and Scope The objective of this study was to improve understanding of the interaction between groundwater and surface water and the effect of groundwater withdrawals on streamflow in the Waihe‘e and Kahalu‘u watersheds. Previous studies have shown that groundwater withdrawals from dike- impounded groundwater settings affect streamflow, but an updated and more precise understanding of the relations between groundwater and surface water in Waihe‘e and Kahalu‘u watersheds is needed to help resource managers balance the needs of competing water uses (Hirashima, 1962, 1963, 1965, 1971; Takasaki and others, 1969). Most studies of the effect of tunnel withdrawals on streamflow in the Waihe‘e and Kahalu‘u watersheds were done prior to the construction of some of the wells currently in use in the watersheds and therefore do not address the effects of these wells. Additionally, groundwater withdrawals from dike- impounded groundwater settings on O‘ahu are known to have affected streams beyond surface- water divides (Hirashima, 1962); therefore, not all withdrawals from wells in a watershed will cause an equivalent reduction in stream base flow within the watershed. The aim of this study was to compile available withdrawal, rainfall, and streamflow data for the Waihe‘e and Kahalu‘u watersheds and use a combination of base- flow- separation, flow- duration, statistical modeling, and other time- series analyses to determine if response to groundwater withdrawals can be detected in streamflow. This study examined historical data for patterns of natural groundwater discharge to streams and possible indications of effects resulting from historical withdrawals. This study also ran withdrawal simulations on an existing, unmodified Oʻahu groundwater model to examine the consequences of groundwater withdrawal, including the reduction of groundwater discharge to springs and streams within and outside the watershed. 4 Groundwater and surface- water interactions in the Waihe‘e- Kahalu‘u watershed, O‘ahu, Hawai‘i Setting The island of Oʻahu is the third largest Hawaiian island with an area of 597 square miles (mi2; Juvik and Juvik, 1998). The island was formed by two shield volcanoes, the eroded remnants of which are the Waiʻanae Range to the west (peak altitude of 4,025 feet [ft]) and the Koʻolau Range to the east (peak altitude of 3,150 ft; fig. 1; Sherrod and others, 2021). Weathering and erosion have greatly modified the volcanoes, carving out large amphitheater valleys and steep interfluvial ridges in the island’s interior. The Waiheʻe- Kahaluʻu watershed occupies an area of about 3.77 mi2 on the northeast flank of the Koʻolau Range. The watershed is bounded by steep cliffs on the southwest and descends into a lowland area that includes wetlands and a small estuary in the northeast. Two principal streams, Waihe‘e and Kahalu‘u Streams, drain the watershed and flow into the estuary, which empties into Kāne‘ohe Bay and the Pacific Ocean. The Hawaiian Islands lie in the trade- wind belt of the tropical North Pacific Ocean. Most areas of the islands have mild temperatures, moderate humidity, and prevailing northeasterly trade winds (Giambelluca and Schroeder, 1998). Precipitation distribution is influenced by the orographic effect—prevailing northeasterly trade winds blow against the mountain slopes, forcing air to rise and cool and water to condense. On O‘ahu, average annual rainfall is higher in areas on northeast- facing (windward) slopes, which include the Waiheʻe- Kahaluʻu watershed and the crest of the Koʻolau Range, and lower on southwest- facing (leeward) slopes. The orographic effect also results in steep rainfall gradients. In the Waiheʻe- Kahaluʻu watershed, rainfall ranges from about 60 inches per year (in/yr) near the coast to more than 140 in/ yr at the ridge crests (Giambelluca and others, 2013; fig. 1). The interaction of trade winds and mountains provides a voluminous and persistent influx of freshwater to Hawai‘i. Without the orographic effect, rainfall would be less than 30 in/yr, similar to that of the open ocean in this region of the Pacific Ocean (Giambelluca and Schroeder, 1998). Occasional convective storms unrelated to the orographic effect can bring rain to any part of the island (Giambelluca and others, 2013). The Waiʻanae and Koʻolau Ranges are the erosional remnants of two shield volcanoes that built the subaerial part of O‘ahu (a third shield volcano is buried by the Waiʻanae volcano, but its rocks do not crop out above sea level; Sinton and others, 2014; Sherrod and others, 2021). The Waiheʻe- Kahaluʻu watershed lies in a part of the Koʻolau Range dominated by the dike- impounded- groundwater setting—one of the principal groundwater settings in Hawai‘i’s volcanic aquifers (Izuka and others, 2018, 2021). In this setting, volcanic dikes intrude into the lava flows that form the bulk of the shield volcano (fig. 2). The hydrology of dike- intruded areas can be conceptualized as a system of compartments— groundwater is stored in compartments of high- permeability lava flows between the low- permeability dikes (Takasaki and Mink, 1985). Because of the impounding effect of the dikes, groundwater can accumulate to high altitudes in the dike compartments. Groundwater can flow from one compartment to another; a small amount of water seeps through the dike rock, but most of the water likely flows over the top or around the dikes (Macdonald and others, 1960). In the Waiheʻe- Kahaluʻu watershed, where erosion has breached the dike compartments, groundwater discharges to springs, streams, wetlands, and the ocean. Most dikes in the Koʻolau Range are aligned in a northwest- southeast trend that parallels the ridge crest, which corresponds to the trend of the ancient rift zone of the Koʻolau shield volcano (fig. 3). Groundwater flows preferentially in the direction parallel to the alignment of the dikes (Hirashima, 1962; Takasaki and Mink, 1985). Groundwater Development Withdrawing dike- impounded groundwater causes water- level declines and reductions in groundwater discharge to streams, springs, wetlands, and the ocean (Izuka and others, 2018). Groundwater in the dike- impounded setting flows from areas of inflow (recharge areas) to areas of natural outflow such as springs, streams, wetlands, and submarine seeps, as well as to artificial withdrawals such as those from wells and tunnels. Prior to groundwater withdrawals, the system can be assumed to be in a near- steady- state condition in which the long- term average inflow rate balances the long- term average outflow rate (fig. 2A). Introduction of withdrawals from wells and tunnels upsets the predevelopment balance, and the system gradually transitions toward a new balance. Water withdrawn artificially initially comes from storage, but over time, more and more of the withdrawal is compensated by reductions in natural groundwater discharge to springs, streams, wetlands, and the ocean. If withdrawals are consistent, the system will eventually achieve a new steady- state condition, storage reduction will cease, and the outflow rate (natural outflow plus artificial withdrawals) will again equal the inflow rate (fig. 2B). Thus, groundwater withdrawal from wells and tunnels eventually will be compensated by an equivalent reduction of natural groundwater discharge to springs, streams, wetlands, or the ocean (assuming recharge remains constant). How far the effect of groundwater withdrawals will spread depends on the withdrawal rates and their locations relative to geologic structures, distribution of hydraulic properties, and sites of natural discharge, such as springs, streams, wetlands, and the ocean (Izuka and Rotzoll, 2023). If withdrawals are subsequently reduced or halted, the system will transition to a new equilibrium in which natural groundwater discharge to streams, springs, wetlands, and the ocean will have increased by an amount equal to the reduction of withdrawal, and storage will also increase (Izuka and others, 2018). The amount of time needed to achieve a new equilibrium depends on recharge, aquifer properties, and the amount of withdrawal reductions. Sometimes, changes in withdrawals occur frequently enough that the steady state cannot be achieved. Variations in precipitation can also cause short- term imbalances between inflows and outflows. Tunnels and Wells in the Waihe‘e- Kahalu‘u Watershed Dike- impounded groundwater in the Waihe‘e- Kahalu‘u watershed has been developed by wells drilled vertically or at an incline into the dike compartments ( fig. 2B). Dike- impounded groundwater is also developed by tunnels driven nearly horizontally into dike compartments. During tunnel construction, water discharges freely from a dike compartment when it is penetrated. The discharge can initially be substantial but diminishes as storage in the dike compartment is reduced (Takasaki and Mink, 1985). Water issuing from the tunnel during construction is commonly allowed to flow into a nearby stream, thereby causing an artificial enhancement of base flow that can be recorded at a streamgage (Hirashima, 1962, 1963). The artificial enhancement of discharge to the stream ceases when the tunnel is completed and the water is directed into pipes. This study assesses the effects of four groundwater developments in the Waihe‘e- Kahalu‘u watershed. The effect of withdrawals from these wells, as well as from tunnels and wells outside the watershed, are discussed in the section, “Changes in Base Flow Relative to Changes in Withdrawal.” Analyses of Historical Data This study used flow- duration, base- flow separation, and double- mass curve analyses of historical streamgage records to assess the groundwater- discharge (base- flow) component of streamflow in the Waihe‘e- Kahalu‘u watershed. The results of these analyses were compared to historical data on groundwater withdrawal to examine changes in groundwater withdrawal and changes in base flow. This study also examined rainfall data because precipitation is the source of groundwater and rainfall fluctuations can cause variations in groundwater recharge, storage, and discharge to streams. Flow- Duration Analysis Flow- duration curves are cumulative- frequency curves that show the percentage of time specified dischar ges were equaled or exceeded during a given period (Searcy, 1959; fig. 7). They can be used to compare base-flow differences for selected periods at a streamgage to identify changes, for instance, before and after withdrawals. Plotted on a log- probability graph, flow- duration curves from gages where streamflow has a substantial groundwater component tend to have a low- flow end (right side) that is less steep than the high- flow end (left side). In contrast, the curves from gages that measure reaches without a substantial groundwater component tend to slope steeply downward. To ensure all seasons are represented equally, many flow- duration curves (including the ones discussed here) are computed based on full water years. A water year is a 12- month period starting on October 1 and ending September 30. Partial water years are excluded from the analysis. Water years during the period of tunnel construction when the base flow of the stream was artificially enhanced were also excluded from the analysis. In this study, several flow- duration curves were constructed using data from streamgages on Waihe‘e and Kahalu‘u Streams for selected key periods in the history of groundwater withdrawals. To facilitate discussion of the various periods (and to distinguish them from periods used in other analyses later in this report), the periods for the flow- duration analyses are given the abbreviated names “FDK#” (for flow- duration analyses using data from Kahalu‘u Stream) and “FDW#” (for flow- duration analyses using data from Waihe‘e Stream), where # is a sequential number unique to the period and stream ( fig. 8). Flow- duration curves were constructed using daily mean streamflow from the record of gage 16283000 on Kahaluʻu Stream for two periods: (1) FDK1, representing the period before construction of the Kahaluu Tunnel; and (2) FDK2, representing the period after construction of the Kahaluu Tunnel ( fig. 8A). The flow- duration curves indicate that base flow at gage 16284000 was the highest in FDW1, intermediate in FDW2, and lowest in FDW3. The apparent decrease in base flow between FDW1 and FDW2 corresponds with the onset of withdrawals from Waihee Tunnel in FDW2. The further decrease in base flow between FDW2 and FDW3 corresponds with the additional withdrawal from the Waihee Incline Wells. The correspondence between withdrawal increases and base- flow decreases in Kahalu‘u and Waihe‘e Streams is consistent with the previously discussed effects of groundwater withdrawals on streamflow in dike- impounded groundwater settings (fig. 2). The flow- duration curves thus provide a graphical analysis of the relative effects of withdrawal on stream base flow, but quantifying base- flow differences among the curves is problematic. Base flow for a given curve is sometimes assumed to be the discharge corresponding to an arbitrary specified exceedance percentage (such as 90 percent), but the percentage corresponding to a specific statistic (such as mean base flow) likely differs among streams. The flow- duration analyses also do not consider non- steady- state conditions within periods or possible effects of rainfall differences among the periods. Because these factors complicate the quantification of the effects of groundwater withdrawals on stream base flow from the flow- duration analyses, quantification is deferred to the base- flow and double- mass- curve analyses discussed later in this report. In this study, base flow was determined from daily mean streamflow data from gages 16283000 and 16283200 on Kahalu‘u Stream and the extended record of gage 16284000 on Waihe‘e Stream ( fig. 1). Base flow was isolated using the standard Base- Flow Index (BFI) base- flow- separation program (Wahl and Wahl, 1995) with a partition window (the N parameter required by BFI) of 5 days and a turning- point test factor (the dimensionless f parameter required by BFI) of 0.9. One assumption of base- flow separation analyses, when used to assess groundwater discharge to streams, is that the data are unaffected by artificial surface- water reservoirs that can release water at rates that can be mistaken for base flow (Barlow and others, 2015). This assumption is valid for the data in this study because no artificial reservoirs exist in the drainage areas of the gages analyzed. Another assumption is that the data reflect contributions from two natural sources. As discussed previously, the flow-duration curves indicate that in the Waihe’e-Kahalu’u watershed, the two sources are direct runoff from rainfall and groundwater discharge. Other assumptions are related to the use of base- flow- separation analysis to estimate water- budget components such as groundwater recharge; these assumptions are not relevant to this study. Changes in Base Flow Relative to Changes in Withdrawal The effect of withdrawals on streams in the Waihe‘e- Kahalu‘u watershed can be estimated from the base- flow- separation analyses by comparing base- flow changes to withdrawal changes. If the periods from these analyses represent steady- state conditions, then the sum of all changes in groundwater discharge (including base flow) would balance the changes in withdrawal. However, the periods likely do not represent steady- state conditions because they include transitions from one withdrawal condition to another as well as variations in rainfall. Withdrawal variations that have the potential to affect streamflow in the Waihe‘e- Kahalu‘u watershed are likely to include not only those from tunnels and wells considered in this study, but also withdrawals from wells in the lower watershed along with tunnels and wells that are outside the watershed (Hirashima, 1962, 1963, 1971)—the timing of the effects is dependent on distance and the hydraulic properties of the rocks. Also, base- flow changes indicated by the analyses only account for the effect on stream reaches upstream from the gages used in this study; they do not account for effects on all reaches, the ocean, or wetlands potentially affected by withdrawals. Even so, comparison of long- term average base- flow changes to withdrawal changes can be used to quantify the general effect of historical withdrawals on some stream reaches in the Waihe‘e- Kahalu‘u watershed (provided that no presumption is made that the quantities represent true steady state). To minimize the possible effects of non- steady withdrawal (at least for wells and tunnels closest to the reaches within the drainage areas of the streamgages), periods when withdrawal was relatively steady were selected for quantitative comparison of withdrawal and base- flow changes. Withdrawal may have fluctuated, but it showed no consistent downward or upward trend within a selected period (figs. 11, 12). Stream base flows in the period may still have been affected by antecedent non- steady withdrawals if substantial lag between withdrawals and their effects on base flow are present, but the close correspondence between withdrawals and base- flow changes (previously discussed in the “Base- Flow Separation” section) indicate that such lags are fairly short in the study area. A double mass curve is a bivariate plot of the cumulative values of two variables that are presumed to covary. If the presumption is valid, the data points in the double- mass curve will tend to plot in a straight line. A change in slope of a double- mass curve indicates a change in how the two variables covary—for example, something caused a change in one variable while leaving the other variable unaffected (Searcy and others, 1960). In this study, double- mass curves were used to compare base flow and rainfall (fig. 13). Annual data by water year were used to reduce the effect of short- term differences and improve the covariation between these datasets (only water years with complete base- flow and rainfall data were analyzed). A factor, such as groundwater withdrawal, that affects base flow but not rainfall, will result in a change in slope of the curve. The double- mass curve in figure 13A compares cumulative annual base flow at streamgage 16283000 on Kahalu‘u Stream to cumulative annual rainfall at the Waiahole 837.00 rain gage for water years 1936–71 (water years 1946 and 1947 were excluded to eliminate artificial base- flow enhancement during the construction of Kahaluu Tunnel; refer to the “Base- Flow Separation” section for more information). The curve shows three breaks in slope; regression lines fit in the data between the breaks have slopes that are statistically different (p is less than [<] 0.05 using the t- test for comparing regression slopes). In each case, the slope after the break is lower than the slope before the break, which indicates successive decreases in base flow. The first decrease occurs in water year 1942, coinciding with the construction of and subsequent withdrawals of about 1.9 Mgal/d (2.9 ft3/s) from Haiku Tunnel (starting in 1941), which is in another watershed 2.3 miles to the southeast of gage 16283000, but is known to have had an effect on flow in Kahalu‘u Stream (Hirashima, 1962, 1963, 1971; Izuka and others, 2024). The second and third decreases occur in water years 1948 and 1960, coinciding with the onset of withdrawals from Kahaluu Tunnel in 1948 (fig. 9) and the substantial increase in withdrawal from Waihee Tunnel between 1960 and 1966 (fig. 10). Numerical Groundwater- Model Simulations As part of this study, an existing numerical groundwater model of O‘ahu was used to simulate how much and where streamflow might increase if selected groundwater withdrawals in the Waihe‘e- Kahalu‘u watershed were shut down. The O‘ahu model is steady state (Izuka and others, 2021), so decreases in withdrawals caused by the shutdown of wells and tunnels would be balanced by increases in groundwater discharge (such as base flow) to streams in the model. Model Description The O‘ahu model was originally created as part of the Hawai‘i V olcanic Aquifer Study (Izuka and Rotzoll, 2023) to assess the consequences of groundwater development on an island- wide scale. The model was created using MODFLOW- 2005 (Harbaugh, 2005) with the Seawater Intrusion Package (SWI2; Bakker and others, 2013) and was calibrated to average conditions during the period 2001–10 (Izuka and others, 2021 ). The single- layer model is divided (discretized) into 500- by 500- ft cells. The active domain of the model represents only the volcanic aquifers of O‘ahu; the effects of coastal- plain sedimentary (“caprock”) units on groundwater flow in the volcanic aquifers are simulated using head- dependent boundaries. Streams were simulated using head- dependent discharge boundaries. While the O‘ahu model was created to meet the needs of a previous study, it has some limitations that bear on the objectives of this study. One limitation is that dikes are not simulated as individual hydrogeologic units. Instead, areas of the model representing dike- impounded groundwater were assigned hydraulic properties (adjusted during calibration) that represent a combination of low- permeability dikes and higher - permeability lava flows. Another limitation of the model is that some stream reaches are not simulated. The model’s streams are based on the National Hydrography Dataset (U.S. Geological Survey, 2012) but reaches on coastal sediments and some minor stream reaches on the volcanic aquifer are omitted. Any simulated effects that may have occurred on the omitted streams are accounted for in the effects on other stream reaches and the ocean. Groundwater withdrawal simulated in the original O‘ahu model by Izuka and others (2021) represents average rates for 2001–10 (fig. 14). Tunnels were simulated using head- dependent discharge boundaries and were allowed to flow without pumping (as they do in reality). During model calibration, the hydraulic properties of the model cells representing the tunnels were adjusted until the simulated tunnel flow matched the tunnels’ average withdrawal rates for 2001–10. Simulated withdrawal rates in the original model included 1.75 Mgal/d (2.71 ft3/s) from Kahaluu Tunnel, 0.68 Mgal/d (1.05 ft3/s) from Kahaluu Well, 4.35 Mgal/d (6.73 ft3/s) from Waihee Tunnel, and 0.98 Mgal/d (1.52 ft3/s) from the Waihee Incline Wells. Simulated Withdrawal Scenarios Seven scenarios simulating shutdown of selected withdrawals from wells and tunnels in the Waihe‘e- Kahalu‘u watershed were run on the O‘ahu model: (1) 0.68 Mgal/d from Kahaluu Well; (2) 1.75 Mgal/d from Kahaluu Tunnel; (3) 2.43 Mgal/d from the combined shutdown of Kahaluu Tunnel and Kahaluu Well; (4) 0.98 Mgal/d from the Waihee Incline Wells; (5) 4.35 Mgal/d withdrawal from Waihee Tunnel; (6) 5.33 Mgal/d from the combined shutdown of Waihee Tunnel and Waihee Well; and (7) 7.76 Mgal/d from the combined shutdown of Kahaluu Well, Kahaluu Tunnel, Waihee Incline Well, and Waihee Tunnel. Results of these simulations were compared to those from the original model to evaluate how the shutdowns affected stream base flows (table 6; figs. 15, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20, and 21). Because the O‘ahu model is steady state, the simulations assess the changes that would ultimately result if the tunnel and (or) well were shut down indefinitely and other conditions (such as recharge and withdrawals from other wells) remained unchanged. The simulated shutdown will be balanced by an equal increase in total groundwater discharge, including groundwater discharge to streams, springs, and the ocean. Shutting down withdrawals from selected tunnels and wells in a model simulation also results in increased inflows to tunnels that were not shut down. For example, in the simulation in which 0.68 Mgal/d of withdrawal from Kahaluu Well was shut down, simulated withdrawals from Kahaluu and Waihee Tunnels increased by 0.06 and 0.07 Mgal/d, respectively, and the total simulated change in withdrawal is –0.55 Mgal/d (table 6). The simulated increases in tunnel withdrawal are the model’s response to the increase in groundwater head (which increased the head difference across the head- dependent boundaries used to simulate the tunnels) resulting from the shutdown of the well. In reality, changes in flow rate from the tunnels would be controlled by the tunnel operator or conditions in the distribution and storage of groundwater after water is taken from the tunnels. If the tunnel withdrawals are artificially prevented from increasing, the simulated tunnel- withdrawal increases would be distributed to other sites of groundwater discharge, such as to streams. Simulation Results Results of the seven simulations indicate that shutdown of withdrawals from wells and tunnels in the Waihe‘e- Kahalu‘u watershed will increase base flows in streams within the watershed as well as in streams northwest, southwest, and southeast along the trend of the Ko‘olau Range (figs. 15, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20, and 21). The distribution of base- flow increases is consistent with preferential groundwater flow parallel to dike orientation in the dike- impounded groundwater of the Ko‘olau Range and is also consistent with previous studies. Hirashima (1962, 1963, 1971) showed that the effects of groundwater withdrawals can spread for miles beyond the topographic divides that define surface watersheds. The simulations also show that shutdown of larger withdrawal will result in farther spread of base- flow increases. Although the steady- state conditions of the O‘ahu model mean that decreases in withdrawals from the shutdown of wells and tunnels in the Waihe‘e- Kahalu‘u watershed will be balanced by an increase in groundwater discharge, not all of the increase will occur in Waihe‘e and Kahalu‘u streams. Simulated shutdown of withdrawal from Kahaluu Well caused a base- flow increase of 0.31 Mgal/d (0.48 ft3/s) in streams within the Waihe‘e- Kahalu‘u watershed. This increase constitutes about 46 percent of the withdrawal rate that was shut down in the simulation (table 6); the remaining 54 percent emerges as increased base flow in streams outside the watershed, groundwater discharge to the wetlands and the ocean, or as increased simulated- tunnel discharge within and outside of the watershed (including the increases of 0.07 Mgal/d [0.11 ft3/s] to Waihee Tunnel and 0.06 Mgal/d [0.09 ft3/s] to Kahaluu Tunnel, as discussed in the “Simulated Withdrawal Scenarios” section. Simulated shutdown of withdrawal from Kahaluu Tunnel caused base flow within the watershed to increase by 0.68 Mgal/d (1.05 ft3/s), or 39 percent of the withdrawal rate, and caused discharge from Waihee Tunnel to increase by 0.23 Mgal/d (0.36 ft3/s).
