'''Zabid''' ({{langx|ar|زَبِيد}}) (also spelled '''Zabīd''', '''Zabeed''' and '''Zebid''') is a town with an urban population of around 52,590 people, located on [[Yemen]]'s western coastal plain. It is one of the oldest towns in Yemen, and has been a [[UNESCO]] [[World Heritage Site]] since 1993. However, in 2000, the site was placed on the [[List of World Heritage in Danger]]. The town was the capital of several ruling dynasties in Yemen over many centuries.

==History==
The town is one of the oldest in Yemen. It was originally a village known as al-Husayb that was inhabited by the Asha'ir tribe.<ref name=":0">{{Encyclopaedia of Islam, Second Edition|volume=11|title=Zabīd|last=Sadek|first=Noha|page=370–371}}</ref> It later took on the name of the [[Wadi]] Zabid, the valley to its south.<ref name=":0" /> According to tradition, the town's early history is associated with [[Abu Musa al-Ash'ari]], one of the [[Companions of the Prophet|companions]] of the Islamic prophet [[Muhammad]], who is said to have built the al-Asha'ir Mosque as the fifth mosque in the [[history of Islam]].<ref>{{Cite web |last= |first= |title=Historic Town of Zabid |url=https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/611/ |access-date=2024-01-09 |website=UNESCO World Heritage Centre |language=en}}</ref> 

The present town was created circa 820 by Muhammad ibn Abdallah ibn Ziyad, the founder of the [[Ziyadid dynasty]], who had been sent by the Abbasid caliph al-Ma'mun to suppress a rebellion of the Asha'ir and Akk tribes.<ref name=":0" /><ref name=":24">{{Cite book |last= |first= |url=https://books.google.com/books?id=un4WcfEASZwC |title=The Grove Encyclopedia of Islamic Art and Architecture |publisher=Oxford University Press |year=2009 |isbn=9780195309911 |editor-last=Bloom |editor-first=Jonathan M. |location= |pages= |language=en |chapter=Zabid |editor-last2=Blair |editor-first2=Sheila S.}}</ref> It became the capital of the Ziyadids (up to 1016) and continued to serve this role under their successors, the [[Najahids|Najahid dynasty]] (from 1022 to 1158) and the [[Mahdids]] (1158 to 1174).<ref name=":0" /> During this time the city grew in importance. Its mosques, including the [[al-Asha'ir Mosque]] and the [[Great Mosque of Zabid|Great Mosque]], became centers of Islamic learning.<ref name=":0" /> Its location on the road between [[Mecca]] and [[Aden]] also granted the city economic prosperity.<ref name=":0" />

In 1067, during the [[hajj|pilgrimage to Mecca]], the [[Najahids|Banu Najah]] clan under Sa'id Ibn Najah, the prince of Zabid, attacked the travel party of the [[Sulayhid]] sultan, [[Ali al-Sulayhi]], and his wife [[Asma bint Shihab]]. They killed Ali and took Asma prisoner. She was sequestered in a secret prison in Zabid, and the severed head of her spouse was reportedly planted on a pole visible from her cell.<ref name="fqi">[[Fatema Mernissi|Mernissi, Fatima]]; Mary Jo Lakeland (2003). ''The Forgotten Queens of Islam.'' Oxford University Press. {{ISBN|978-0-19-579868-5}}.</ref> After a year's imprisonment, she managed to get a message through to her son and daughter-in-law in [[Sanaa]], and her son stormed Zabid and freed her.

Ali ibn Mahdi, a native of the Yemeni highlands, founded the [[Mahdids|Mahdid dynasty]] in the [[Tihama]] region. Al-Himyari and his followers burned down several districts north of Zabid. He had sworn to put the Abyssinians into slavery and ordered his men to kill everyone, including the handicapped.<ref>{{cite book|author=Henry Cassels Kay|title=Yaman its early medieval history|year=1999|page=127|publisher=Adegi Graphics LLC|isbn=1421264641}}</ref> Out of desperation, the people of Zabid sought assistance from the [[Imams of Yemen|Zaydi imam]] [[Al-Mutawakkil Ahmad bin Sulayman|Ahmed ibn Sulayman]] against al-Himyari. The Zaydi imam ordered [[Fatiq III]] to be executed on account of his alleged [[homosexuality]]. Fatiq III was either killed by the Imam, the Mahdids, or his own soldiers. With this event, the slave dynasty came an end and the Mahdids took over Zabid in 1158.

After the [[Ayyubids]] took control of Yemen in 1174, Zabid continued to be the economic and political center of the region.<ref name=":0" /> Under the [[Rasulid dynasty]] (1229–1454), the official capital was moved to [[Ta'izz]] but the rulers continued to use Zabid as a winter residence. The city reached its apogee during this period and had a larger number of palaces, gardens, and religious institutions than any other city in Yemen.<ref name=":0" /> [[ʿAlī ibn al-Ḥasan al-Khazrajī|Al-Khazraji]], a local 14th-century Arab writer, claimed that the city counted between 230 and 240 mosques.<ref name=":0" /> Even today, the city reportedly has the highest concentration of mosques in Yemen.<ref name="aljazeera">{{cite news |title=War ‘at the gates’ of Zabid UNESCO World Heritage |url=https://www.aljazeera.com/features/2018/3/6/war-at-the-gates-of-zabid-unesco-world-heritage |access-date=11 January 2024 |work=Al Jazeera |date=6 March 2018}}</ref>

[[Hadım Suleiman Pasha]] extended the [[Ottoman Empire]]'s authority to include Zabid in 1539.<ref name=":1" /> Zabid became the administrative headquarters of [[Yemen Eyalet]].<ref name=":1">{{cite book |author=Muḥammad ibn Aḥmad Nahrawālī|title=Lightning Over Yemen: A History of the Ottoman Campaign in Yemen, 1569–71|trans-title=البرق اليماني في الفتح العثماني |year=2002 |publisher=OI.B.Tauris |isbn=1860648363 |page=88 |language=ar}}</ref>

[[File:Mausoleum syedna Mohammad Ejuddin.JPG|thumb|right|Mausoleum Muhammad Ezzuddin, Zabid, Yemen]]

Today, however, Zabid is at the economic margins of modern Yemen.<ref>Eickelman, Dale F. "The Middle East and Central Asia." (Prentice Hall, Upper Saddle River: 2002)</ref> [[Muhammad Abdul-Wali]]'s novel ''[[Sana'a: An Open City]]'' tells the story of a young man who traveled to Zabid in the mid-20th century and was surprised to find the town had become a backwater. Some descendants of the [[Rasulid dynasty|Rasulids]] continue to use Zabid alongside their [[Nobiliary particle|nobiliary particles]].<ref>{{cite news |title=NJCU Receives Largest Estate Gift in University History |url=https://njbmagazine.com/njb-news-now/njcu-receives-largest-estate-gift-in-university-history/ |access-date=11 September 2025 |work=New Jersey Business Magazine |date=22 December 2022}}</ref>

==Geography==
Zabid has an urban population of around 52,590 persons. It is located on Yemen's western coastal plain, in the Tihama region.<ref>[http://www.geonames.org/maps/google_14.195_43.315.html population of Zabid]</ref>

==World Heritage Site==
Zabid was declared a [[World Heritage Site]] by [[UNESCO]] in 1993.<ref>{{cite web|url=https://whc.unesco.org/en/decisions/3362|work=unesco.org|title=Decision : CONF 002 XI Inscription: Historic Town of Zabid (Yemen)|date=1993}}</ref> Zabid's Great Mosque occupies a prominent place in the town. The vestiges of its university can also be visited. In 2000, Zabid was listed on the [[List of World Heritage in Danger]]; the listing was made at the behest of the Yemeni government due to a state of poor upkeep and conservation. According to a [[UNESCO]] report, roughly "40% of the city's houses have been replaced by concrete buildings, and other houses and the ancient souk are in a deteriorating state."<ref>[https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/611/threats/ UNESCO World Heritage Site in Danger 2000: Historic Town of Zabid]</ref><ref>{{cite news
|url=http://www.sabanews.net/en/news176233.htm
|title=Is Yemen Able to Keep Zabid Listed in World Heritage?
|author=Ahmad al-Aghbari and Mohammad al-Ulofi
|publisher=Saba News
|date=February 15, 2009
}}</ref> The ongoing [[Yemeni civil war (2014–present)|Yemeni civil war]] also poses a threat to Zabid's heritage. Some historic homes were damaged by [[Saudi-led intervention in the Yemeni civil war|coalition]] bombing in 2015.<ref name=":3">{{Cite web |last=Fenton-Harvey |first=Jonathan |date=2018-03-06 |title=Yemen War Erodes the Country's Architectural Heritage |url=https://theglobepost.com/2018/03/06/yemen-war-heritage/ |access-date=2024-02-21 |website=The Globe Post |language=en-US}}</ref> Further concerns were raised in 2018 as fighting drew near to the city.<ref>{{Cite web |title=War ‘at the gates’ of Zabid UNESCO World Heritage |url=https://www.aljazeera.com/features/2018/3/6/war-at-the-gates-of-zabid-unesco-world-heritage |access-date=2024-02-21 |website=Al Jazeera |language=en}}</ref><ref name=":3" />

==Economy==
As of 1920, Zabid was one of two places in [[Arabia]] growing [[Indigofera tinctoria|indigo]]. Zabid also grows and produces [[cotton]].<ref name=Prothero85>{{cite book|last=Prothero|first=G.W.|title=Arabia|year=1920|publisher=H.M. Stationery Office|location=London|page=85|url=http://www.wdl.org/en/item/11767/view/1/85/}}</ref> The [[British people|British]] cite tribal disputes as dampening the economy in Zabid during the early 20th century.<ref name=Prothero103>{{cite book|last=Prothero|first=G.W.|title=Arabia|year=1920|publisher=H.M. Stationery Office|location=London|page=103|url=http://www.wdl.org/en/item/11767/view/1/103/}}</ref>
