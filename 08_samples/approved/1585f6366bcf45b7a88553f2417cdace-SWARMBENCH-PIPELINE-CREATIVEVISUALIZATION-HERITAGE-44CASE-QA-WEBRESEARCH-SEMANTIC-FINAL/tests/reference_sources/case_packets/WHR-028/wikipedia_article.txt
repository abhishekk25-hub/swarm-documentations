The '''Aïr Mountains''' or '''Aïr Massif'''<ref>The [[diaeresis (diacritic)|diaresis]] over the [[i]] notes that it should be pronounced separately and not together with the [[a]]. Occasionally also spelled without it as '''Air''' or the '''Air of Niger'''.</ref> ([[Air Tamajeq language|Tamajăq]]: ''Ayǝr''; [[Hausa language|Hausa]]:&nbsp;<small>Eastern</small>&nbsp;''Azbin'', <small>Western</small>&nbsp;''Abzin'') is a triangular [[massif]], located in northern [[Niger]], within the [[Sahara]]. Part of the [[West Saharan montane xeric woodlands]] [[ecoregion]], they rise to more than {{convert|1800|m|ft|abbr=on}} and extend over {{convert|84000|km2|sqmi|abbr=on}}.<ref>{{cite journal |last=Thorp |first=M. B. |year=1969 |title=Some aspects of the geomorphology of the Air Mountains, southern Sahara |journal=Transactions of the Institute of British Geographers |volume=47 |issue=47 |pages=25–46 |doi=10.2307/621733|jstor=621733 }}</ref> Lying in the midst of desert north of the [[17th parallel north|17th parallel]], the Aïr plateau, with an average altitude between {{convert|500|and|900|m|ft|abbr=on}}, forms an island of [[Sahel]] climate which supports a wide variety of life, many pastoral and farming communities, and dramatic geological and archaeological sites. There are notable archaeological excavations in the region that illustrate the prehistoric past of this region.<ref>{{cite book |first1=Thurstan |last1=Shaw |first2=Paul |last2=Sinclair |first3=Bassey |last3=Andah |first4=Alex |last4=Okpoko |year=1995 |title=The Archaeology of Africa: Food, Metals and Towns |publisher=Routledge |isbn=0-415-11585-X }}</ref> The endangered [[African wild dog]] (''Lycaon pictus'') once existed in this region, but may now be [[Local extinction|extirpated]] due to [[habitat fragmentation|human population pressures]] in this region.<ref>C. Michael Hogan. 2009. [http://globaltwitcher.auderis.se/artspec_information.asp?thingid=35993 ''Painted Hunting Dog: Lycaon pictus'', GlobalTwitcher.com, ed. N. Stromberg] {{webarchive |url=https://web.archive.org/web/20101209234758/http://globaltwitcher.auderis.se/artspec_information.asp?thingid=35993 |date=December 9, 2010 }}</ref>

==Geology==
[[File:1997 278-13 Sahara Niger.jpg|thumb|left|Desert near Agadez, with the Aïr Mountains in the distance. Note the volcanic outcrops, common to the Aïr.]]
The [[Precambrian]] to [[Cenozoic]] Aïr Mountains consist of [[peralkaline]] [[granite]] intrusions which appear dark in colour (unusual since most granitic masses are light-toned in the field). In the Sahara Desert, such mountains often stand out in stark relief as topographic heights amidst lowlands covered by sand.<ref>[rst.gsfc.nasa.gov/Sect17/Sect17_3.html Use of Remote Sensing in Basic Science Studies], Section 17, [[NASA]]/Primary Author: Nicholas M. Short, Sr. (Site last updated: June 18, 2007).</ref> The terrain consists of high plateau, mountain ranges, and broad, sandy valleys and seasonal wadis which once contained rivers. Areas of these deep, often intersecting, valleys also contain waterborne clay and silt deposits.  Underground watercourses in some of these valleys continue to provide year-round oasis and seasonal vegetation.
[[File:G06 s65 63158.jpg|thumb|Circular granite massifs (dark areas). A volcanic crater can be seen at the lower left. NASA image approximately {{convert|130|km|mi|abbr=on}} across.]]

The Aïr mountains themselves consist of nine almost circular massifs rising from a rocky plateau, bordered by the sand dunes and plain of the [[Ténéré|Ténéré Desert]] to the east. The massif is a [[plateau]] consisting of a sub-[[Cambrian]] age [[erosion surface]] on Precambrian [[metamorphic rock]]s, punctuated by a series of flat-topped, granite intrusion peaks, which include [[Mont Idoukal-n-Taghès]] (Niger's highest point at 2022&nbsp;m),<ref>{{cite peakbagger |pid=11062 |name=Mont Bagzane, Niger}}: incorrectly calls peak Mont Bagzane.  Name of the peak is "Idoukal-n-Taghès", part of the plateau named "Bagzane". Also note, official and international sources believed Mont Greboun to be the highest peak as late as 2001.</ref> [[Mont Tamgak]] (1988&nbsp;m), [[Mont Greboun]] (1944&nbsp;m),<ref>{{cite peakbagger |pid=11061 |name=Mont Greboun, Niger}}.</ref> [[Adrar Bous]], [[Fadei]], [[Chirriet]], [[Taghmert]], [[Agueraguer]], [[Takaloukouzet]], and [[Goundai]].

The massif contains [[volcanic]] features including the extinct [[caldera]] of Arakao, [[Cenozoic]] lava flows of [[hawaiite]] to [[trachyte]] composition, [[volcanic cone]]s, [[tuff]] rings and one of the largest [[Ring dike|ring dike system]]s in the world.<ref>Paul D. Lowman Jr. [http://volcano.oregonstate.edu/volcanoes/volc_images/africa/air.html VolcanoWorld: Air, Niger] {{webarchive |url=https://web.archive.org/web/20090814103013/http://volcano.oregonstate.edu/volcanoes/volc_images/africa/air.html |date=August 14, 2009 }}. North Dakota and Oregon Space Grant Consortia, administered by the Department of Geosciences at Oregon State University.</ref> At Izouzaoenehe, lie the [[marble]] [[Blue Mountains (Niger)|Blue Mountains]], and the lower [[Zagado Valley]] is surrounded by white marble hills. [[Carboniferous]] [[sandstone]] and [[coal]] units in the [[Iullemmeden Basin]] just to the west of the massif contain [[uranium]] mineralisation sourced from the granites of the massif.<ref>[http://www.nwturanium.com/i/pdf/Uranium-Niger-Analyst.pdf URANIUM GEOLOGY: NIGER, WEST AFRICA] {{webarchive |url=https://web.archive.org/web/20090305231404/http://www.nwturanium.com/i/pdf/Uranium-Niger-Analyst.pdf |date=March 5, 2009 }}, NWT Uranium Corp.</ref>

==Climate==
[[File:Timia Oasis.JPG|thumb|right|The [[Guelta]] near the town of [[Timia]] in central Aïr provides year-round water in an otherwise dry region.]]
Because of its altitude (on average between 500 and 900&nbsp;m) and despite its low rainfall (50 to 160&nbsp;mm/year on the lower plateau), the Aïr forms a green region in comparison with the surrounding deserts, especially after the August–September seasonal rains. The climate is classified as [[Sahel]], like that of the regions well to its south.  While the mountains are largely bare of vegetation, the dry [[wadi]] river valleys (known by the [[Hausa language|Hausa]] term "''Kori''") channel and hold rainwater in [[guelta]]s (stone pools, such as that near the town of [[Timia]]), creating oases which provide forage for animals, and in some areas, farming.  The high Bagzane plateau of the central Aïr in particular provides adequate rainfall for intensive agriculture.  Other, vast, areas  of the region are entirely devoid of plant life and with their volcanic protrusions and [[Hamada|rock fields]] present an otherworldly appearance.

==Vegetation==
More than 430 vascular species have been recorded so far in the Aïr mountains.<ref>Bruneau de Miré, P. & Gillet, H. 1956. Contribution à l'étude de la flore du massif de l'Aïr – Première partie. Journal d'Agronomie Tropicale et de Botanique Appliquée 3,422–438</ref> The location of the Aïr as a southern extension of the Hoggar mountain range makes it a connection between the Saharan Flora and the Sahelian Flora.<ref>Ozenda, P., 2004. Flore du Sahara, third ed. CNRS, Paris. {{ISBN|2-271-06230-6}}</ref> However, the presence of mountains up to 2000 m a.s.l. generates locally favourable conditions for several species of the Sudanian zone and the Mediterranean zone.<ref>Poilecot in Giazzi, F., 1996. Étude initiale – la Réserve Naturelle Nationale de l'Aïr et du Ténéré (Niger) – connaissance des éléments du milieu naturel et humain dans le cadre d'orientations pour un aménagement et une conservation durables – analyse descriptive. MH/E, WWF, UICN, Gland, Suisse, 712 pages</ref>

During the 20th century a series of scientific missions in the Aïr has permitted to identify the majority of plant species developing in the Aïr.<ref>{{cite journal | last1 = Aubréville | first1 = A | year = 1938 | title = La forêt coloniale – les forêts de l'Afrique occidentale française | journal = Ann. Acad. Sci. Coloniales | volume = 9 | pages = 1–244 }}</ref><ref>Peyre de Fabrègues, B. & Lebrun, J.P., 1976. Catalogue des Plantes Vasculaires du Niger. IEMVT, Maisons Alfort</ref><ref>Bruneau de Miré, P. & Gillet, H. 1956. Contribution à l'étude de la flore du massif de l'Aïr – Première partie. Journal d'Agronomie Tropicale et de Botanique Appliquée 3,422–438</ref> ''[[Vachellia tortilis]]'', subsp. ''raddiana'' (''afagag'') and ''[[Balanites aegyptiaca]]'' (''aborak{{which lang|date=October 2022}}'') are among the most frequent tree species in the intermountain zone. In the vicinity of temporary rivers named ''koris'', species like ''[[Vachellia nilotica]]'', ''[[Faidherbia albida]]'' and the palm ''[[Hyphaene thebaica]]'' coexist with [[date palm]] cultivars (''Phoenix dactylifera''). Severe droughts and high aridity have made the intermountain zone of the Aïr a particularly harsh place for plants to develop.<ref>Poilecot in Giazzi, F., 1996. Étude initiale – la Réserve Naturelle Nationale de l'Aïr et du Ténéré (Niger) – connaissance des éléments du milieu naturel et humain dans le cadre d'orientations pour un aménagement et une conservation durables – analyse descriptive. MH/E, WWF, UICN, Gland, Suisse, 712 pages</ref> The additional presence of domestic herbivores has led to a severe deficit in tree regeneration, which has been cited as a major ecological concern.<ref>Fabien Anthelme, Maman Waziri Mato, Dimitri de Boissieu et Franck Giazzi 2006. http://vertigo.revues.org/2224</ref> Tree regeneration has been observed enhanced as soon as tree seedlings are protected by large tussocks of the frequent grass ''[[Panicum turgidum]]''.<ref>{{cite journal | last1 = Anthelme | first1 = F. | last2 = Michalet | first2 = R. | year = 2009 | title =  Grass-to-tree facilitation in an arid grazed environment (Aïr Mountains, Sahara)| journal = Basic and Applied Ecology| volume = 10| issue = 5| pages = 437–446| doi = 10.1016/j.baae.2008.10.008 }}</ref> This positive interaction between plants represents a promising restoration tool to be used by local inhabitants.

In comparison, mountainous areas are even less documented. Tropical tree species less resistant to drought have been described in the highlands, among which the Fabaceae ''[[Senegalia laeta]]'' and ''[[Vachellia seyal]]''.<ref>{{cite journal | last1 = Aubréville | first1 = A | year = 1938 | title = La forêt coloniale – les forêts de l'Afrique occidentale française | journal = Ann. Acad. Sci. Coloniales | volume = 9 | pages = 1–244 }}</ref> Quezel <ref>Quézel, P., 1965. La végétation du Sahara, du Tchad à la Mauritanie. Fischer Verlag, Stuttgart, Germany</ref> has observed the remnant presence of a rare endemic taxon related to the [[olive]] in the northern sector of the Aïr range. Recently, this taxon, ''Olea europaea'' subsp. ''laperrinei'', has been found in other mountains of the Aïr: these very isolated, small populations represent the southern limit of the species distribution.<ref>Anthelme, F., Abdoulkader, A. & Besnard, G. 2008. https://doi.org/10.1007%2Fs11258-007-9386-6</ref>

A study led on the slopes of the highest summit in the Aïr, [[Mont Idoukal-n-Taghès]] (2022 m a.s.l.), identified plant species that had never been inventoried in Niger before.<ref>{{cite journal | last1 = Anthelme | first1 = F. | last2 = Waziri Mato | first2 = M. | last3 = Maley | first3 = J. | year = 2008 | title =  Elevation and local refuges ensure persistence of mountain specific vegetation in the Nigerien Sahara| journal = Journal of Arid Environments| volume = 72| issue = 12| pages = 2232–2242| doi = 10.1016/j.jaridenv.2008.07.003 | bibcode = 2008JArEn..72.2232A }}</ref> Among them, ''[[Pachycymbium decaisneanum]]'', ''[[Cleome aculeata]]'', ''[[Echinops mildbraedii]]'' and ''[[Indigofera nummularia]]'' are tropical species with relatively low resistance to water stress, whereas ''[[Silene lynesii]]'', ''[[Tephrosia elegans]]'', and ''[[Echinops mildbraedii]]'' have a Saharan-Mediterranean distribution. Three ferns were found for the first time in the Aïr recently, ''[[Cheilanthes coriacea]]'', ''[[Actiniopteris radiata]]'', and ''[[Ophioglossum polyphyllum]]'', suggesting that ferns may be more prone to develop in arid environments than commonly proposed.<ref>{{cite journal | last1 = Anthelme | first1 = F. | last2 = Abdoulkader | first2 = A. | last3 = Viane | first3 = R. | year = 2011 | title =  Are ferns in arid environments underestimated? Contribution from the Saharan Mountains| journal = Journal of Arid Environments| volume = 75| issue = 6| pages = 516–523| doi = 10.1016/j.jaridenv.2011.01.009 | bibcode = 2011JArEn..75..516A }}</ref> All these data evidence a marked mountain climatic specificity in the Aïr, with a positive impact on species richness and species diversity. Because of their strong geographic isolation within a Saharan matrix, these species have a high conservation value.<ref>{{cite journal | last1 = Anthelme | first1 = F. | last2 = Waziri Mato | first2 = M. | last3 = Maley | first3 = J. | year = 2008 | title =  Elevation and local refuges ensure persistence of mountain specific vegetation in the Nigerien Sahara| journal = Journal of Arid Environments| volume = 72| issue = 12| pages = 2232–2242| doi = 10.1016/j.jaridenv.2008.07.003 | bibcode = 2008JArEn..72.2232A }}</ref>

==Population==
The town of [[Agadez]] in the heart of the [[Tuareg people|Tuareg]] country is the capital of Aïr. Much of the Tuareg population of Aïr until recently led a [[nomad]]ic life, relying essentially on camel and goats from which they take milk, meat and skins used in the production of local handicrafts.  Most sedentary populations were either dependents of higher caste Tuareg pastoralists or the [[Ikelan]] (''Bouzou'' in Hausa / ''Bella'' in [[Songhay languages|Songhai]]), Tuareg slaves. These peoples were settled in northern oases, to tend the [[date palm]] plantations held by the noble clans.

Agriculture products from oases such as [[Timia]], [[Aouderas]] and [[Tabelot]] are traditionally exchanged against clothes, or salt, brought by camel caravans ([[Azalai]]) from the remote Tenere oases of [[Bilma]] and [[Fachi]] to the east.

==History==
[[File:Air massif sat map south.png|left|thumb|upright=1.3|Map of the southern Aïr Mountains.]]
The Aïr is known for its [[rock art]], dating from 6000 BCE to around 1000CE.  During the [[African humid period]], the region was a [[pastoral]] area, as is illustrated by images of cattle and large mammals. During the 3rd millennium BC, however, a process of [[desertification]] began and the [[Tuareg people|Tuareg]] from further north migrated into the region. Later art indicated war, depicting [[horse]]s and [[chariot]]s.  In particular, the five-meter-high carvings of the "[[Dabous Giraffes]]" discovered in 1999 is internationally famous. Cave art in the region is predominantly [[stone carving]], initially with sharp rock, and from around 1200 BC perhaps with metal.<ref>Trust for African Rock Art. ROCK ART OF SAHARA AND NORTH AFRICA:THEMATIC STUDY.  [http://www.icomos.org/studies/rockart-sahara-northafrica/07sous-zone3.pdf David COULSON. Sub-Zone 3: Niger] (June 2007).</ref>

When the Tuareg tribes were pushed south by Arab invaders in the eighth and ninth centuries, there were [[Gobir]]wa [[Hausa people|Hausa]] in the southern Aïr.  Successive Tuareg ''Kel''s have controlled the area since at least the twelfth century. Agadez, as well as [[In-Gall]] to the east, were the farthest outposts of the [[Songhai Empire]] in the early 15th century.  In the sixteenth century, the area fell under the newly created Tuareg [[Sultanate of Agadez]], and remained so until the arrival of the [[French West Africa|French]] at the end of the 19th.

The emergence of the French weakened the Tuareg Kels and provoked both infighting and resistance to colonialism.  From the 1880s, Toubu raids increased, and when the Tuareg [[Ag Mohammed Wau Teguidda Kaocen]] rose against the French in 1917, many towns were destroyed on his way to the siege of Agadez.  When the French retook Agadez, a brutal [[punitive expedition]] through the Aïr left many formerly populous places abandoned, razed by Kaosen and the French successively.<ref>Jolijn Geels. Niger. Bradt London and Globe Pequot New York (2006). {{ISBN|1-84162-152-8}}</ref>

While the Kel Owey continued to dominate the settled oasis towns and pastoral herding, the sedentary farmers (Tuareg, Hausa, or Songhai) expanded farming and sedentary livestock cultivation in the mid-20th century.

The famines of the 1970s and 1980s brought an end to this expansion, and as [[Agadez]] and [[Arlit]] grew, the towns of the Aïr have shrunk.  The [[Tuareg rebellion (1990–1995)|first Tuareg rebellion]] of 1990-95 saw brutal government reprisals which depopulated many villages in the Aïr.  Peace from the mid-1990s, as well as the uranium mines of [[Arlit]] brought unprecedented growth to the region, with many small towns gaining valuable tourism revenue.  In 2004, a locust invasion ravaged many gardens, bringing scarcity and contributing to the [[Tuareg rebellion (2007–2009)|Second Tuareg Rebellion]] of 2007–2009. Unrest continues in the region, effectively ending the nascent tourist industry.<ref>Geels (2006)</ref>
